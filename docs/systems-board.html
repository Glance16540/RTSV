Town and economy 5

Gathering

Working

Villagers bring in food, wood, gold and stone.

  • Berries, farms, hunting and fishing for food
  • Trees, gold and stone to work
  • Storehouses as drop-off points

Gaps

  • Every rate and cost is a guess: nobody has played it for balance

Building and walls

Working

Raise a town and fortify it.

  • 20 buildings across two build pages
  • Palisades, stone walls, gates and towers; drag to draw walls
  • A Wonder that wins the game if it stands

Gaps

  • Buildings need nearly level ground, but nothing tells you why a spot is refused

Ages and research

Working

Advance through five ages and buy upgrades.

  • 5 ages from Stone to Gunpowder
  • 21 upgrades: tools, weapons, armour, unit lines

Gaps

  • The research list is short and the same for every nation

Roads

Working, rough

Every building is joined to its town centre.

  • A road is laid the moment a building is placed, and joins nearby roads into streets
  • Units move faster on roads and their routes prefer them
  • Dirt tracks become cobbles from the Iron Age
  • Saved with the game

Gaps

  • You cannot lay or remove a road yourself
  • Roads do not join one town to another
  • Only checked on level ground

Market

Working, rough

Buy and sell resources for gold.

  • Buying and selling at the Market
  • For the Traders (Persians, Abbasids) a Market earns gold by itself, more for every other people that keeps one

Gaps

  • No trade routes or caravans to be seen or raided: the Traders' markets earn by rule, with nothing on the road
  • No trade between players beyond gifts to an ally

The leader on the field

Working, rough

Your nation's leader fights in person: made in a creator, hardened by battle, shaped by the town's piety.

  • A hero creator, opened from the menu and from the campaign map: name, man or woman, calling (Warlord or Strategist), six points across Might, Guard and Vigour, and a turning figure to dress: hair, cloak, headgear, build, beard
  • One leader is kept for each nation on this device and leads it in every skirmish and campaign
  • The first hero of their calling raised in a battle is the leader, by name; the other calling is a plain lieutenant
  • Veterancy: enemies felled within the leader's reach are their deeds, and deeds raise them through five ranks, Blooded to Legend
  • Each rank adds health, and strength or armour as chosen in the creator: all to the arm, all to armour, or the two by turns
  • They are still flesh: however armoured, a leader takes at least three tenths of every blow, and one who falls is raised again a rank lower
  • Starting a campaign asks for your people and who leads them: the nation's leader as written, that leader's other self, or one of your own making
  • Every leader keeps a house: one child half grown at the start, another every five turns while the leader lives, up to three; an heir comes of age at six turns
  • When your leader falls you are asked who follows: an heir of age keeps the parent's gifts and the house, and begins with half their veterancy; or a captain from an army that has won a battle takes command where they stand with that army's veterancy, and the old house ends
  • You may decide later, or hire a stranger at the capital as before; the stranger is a new person and ends the house
  • Rival peoples settle their own succession the same way: eldest heir of age, else their best captain, else a hired stranger
  • Births and successions are written in the chronicle, and the capital's panel names the leader and the heirs
  • In a battle the dead stay dead: the one raised after the leader falls is their heir by name (the next number), a rank lower
  • The call: from Seasoned a leader has one power to set off by hand, from their panel, once every ninety seconds. A Warlord sounds the charge and everyone of theirs within reach strikes a third faster for twelve seconds; a Strategist bids them hold fast and they take 30% less harm
  • Played through by the game's own commands in tests/leader-play.test.ts: a town advances an age and trains its leader; trained spearmen are sent against a work party and one makes his name with four kills while the leader, standing by, becomes Blooded; the leader is ridden down by knights; the named spearman is raised and leads with a Blooded Warlord's battle cry
  • Also played there: a campaign siege fought to its end by computer players on both sides, whose leader, survivors and best soldier (a crossbowman, nine kills) are handed home to the map; and a leader lost on the march at turn 2, followed by their son at turn 5
  • Soldiers make a name: every soldier's kills are counted, and one on foot or horse who has felled three is known by name from then on
  • When your leader has fallen in a battle, such a soldier can be raised on the shields from their panel: they become the leader where they stand with a rank for every three felled, and the old line ends
  • One raised from the ranks goes on fighting as what they were: a knight leads from the saddle, an archer from behind the line, with a leader's presence about them and the Warlord's powers to choose from. They take better arms when their kind does, and in a campaign their army brings them to the next battle the same way
  • One soldier in four who makes a name is a woman, named and addressed as one when raised
  • In a campaign the soldier who felled most and lived is remembered with the army, and is the captain a succession offers, by name and deeds; a soldier raised in the battle itself leads on the map afterwards
  • A leader who fell in a campaign battle is counted as fallen even if another hero was raised later in it
  • Powers: at Blooded, Veteran and Legend a leader comes into one of two powers for their calling. Each can be chosen in the creator or left open; one left open is chosen on the field when its rank is earned, from two buttons on the leader's panel, and cannot be changed after. What was chosen in a campaign battle goes home with the leader, and in a skirmish is kept with them. A Warlord's are a stronger battle cry or a longer reach; health from every blow or a shield against shot; dread that weakens enemies or a fury that quickens as they are wounded. A Strategist has six of their own
  • A power changes the leader on the field the moment the rank is reached, is named in the message and listed on their panel, and survives a saved game
  • A skirmish leader keeps the rank they finish a battle with, and brings it and its powers to the next; if they fell, it is their heir who is kept
  • Each rival people's leader is built to suit how it lives (raiders all arm, builders all armour, and so on) with powers of its own, in skirmish and campaign alike; six peoples have a making of their own, so no two who live alike are led alike, and Babylon's and Greece's leaders are Strategists
  • On the campaign map a leader adds half a point of strength per rank to the army they march with, in attack, in defence, in what the panel shows and in what rivals reckon before they march
  • Whoever leads a people now is named wherever the leader is: beside their speeches, in the list of peoples, in demands, truces and alliances, and beside your own leader's words in battle; a rival's succession is told in the log and the chronicle
  • A holy town's leader lends 1 armour to everyone of theirs nearby, 2 if the town is Righteous
  • A decadent town's leader binds enemy villagers in half a Slaver's time and leads them to a Work Camp; while the leader is in the field the camp's women fill the town two and a half times as fast
  • In the campaign the leader's rank is kept from battle to battle, rises with a battle won, and drops one when they fall
  • Rival nations' leaders take the field too, as written, with their points spread evenly
  • The leader's panel shows rank, deeds to the next, and what the town gives them

Gaps

  • Two callings only
  • Choosing a power on the field is checked by tests only: its two buttons have not been seen in a browser, and nothing but a line in the messages and on the panel tells the player a choice is waiting
  • A rival always has its powers chosen beforehand; a leader of yours fought by the computer (a reserve) takes the first of each pair
  • One power only is set off by hand, the call; a rival's leader raises theirs the moment they are struck at, with no thought for when it would tell most
  • Veterancy comes fast: in the one siege played through by computer players a leader went from Untried to Legend in nineteen minutes. The deeds asked for each rank are too few for a long battle
  • Every number here is a first guess: points, ranks, the armour lent, the camp's rate
  • The leader speaks no lines of their own on the field
  • Rival leaders never take captives when decadent and are not kept out of danger: the computer marches them with the army like any other soldier
  • A Strategist's people (Babylon, Greece) have no leader on the field until the computer raises a Strategist, an age later than a Warlord; whether that weakens them has not been measured
  • A skirmish leader's rank is kept only when the battle is fought to its end, and only on this device; nothing stops it climbing to Legend against Easy rivals
  • Heirs have no mother, no marriages and no rival claims: a house is a list of children and nothing more
  • A new leader speaks the old one's lines, so a successor may still give the dead leader's name in their own words; portraits and recorded voices are the old leader's too
  • Only the one best soldier of a battle is remembered with the army, and only until its next battle or until it joins another army; a battle settled on the map remembers nobody
  • A soldier raised to lead has the same making and a Warlord's powers whatever they fought as; a woman among the soldiers is drawn like the men until she is raised; a leader on horseback or with a bow in the leader's cloak has not been looked at
  • A skirmish forgets a soldier raised in it: the leader you made is kept instead
  • Rival peoples never raise a soldier in battle: when their leader falls there they wait for the map
  • Raising a soldier is reached by the game's own commands in a test (see has), and its button was seen once in a browser with a soldier set up by hand; no person has done it
  • Succession is reached by campaign commands in a test (see has) and its dialog was seen once in a browser on an edited save; no person has played to one
  • Powers count for nothing when a battle is settled on the map: only the rank does
  • The call, the powers, rival builds and kept skirmish rank are checked by tests and one look at the creator; none has been used by a person
  • Face, skin and clothing colours other than the cloak cannot be chosen
  • The creator has been seen in pictures on a slow test renderer, not used by a person

Faith and vice 6

Piety and decadence

Working, rough

A town drifts between Righteous and Decadent, with boons and banes each way.

  • Five standings, each changing births, plague, gold, work and more
  • A town looks its standing: church bright or in ruins, tavern plain or hung with lanterns, people and houses drawn modest or gaudy
  • 9 dilemmas that ask you to choose
  • Each nation starts with its own leaning

Gaps

  • Only 9 dilemmas, so they repeat
  • The numbers behind every boon and bane are guesses
  • Which way each nation leans is my guess, not yours

Church

Working, rough

Villagers serve as monks and nuns; the town grows pious.

  • Up to four clergy per Church
  • More children, shorter plagues, some plagues prevented
  • A holy town trains Priests and refuses pagan units

Gaps

  • Monks and nuns do the same thing: no difference between men and women here, unlike the Tavern
  • No feast days
  • A Church near a Tavern has no effect on it

Tavern

Working, rough

Milkmaids bring gold, patrons drink; the town grows decadent.

  • Women become milkmaids (gold), men become patrons (faster soldiers)
  • Mercenaries for hire, who leave if unpaid
  • Slower work, fewer children, plague in a worldly town

Gaps

  • No rumours about rival towns
  • No brawls or debts

Slavers and work camps

Working, rough

A decadent town steals villagers from others.

  • Slavers seize enemy villagers and march them to a Work Camp
  • Captive men raise the whole town's output
  • Captive women add villagers
  • A rescue in progress is shown to both sides over the camp and on its panel, with the seconds until the next captive is out
  • Rescue: soldiers of the town that lost them, or of its allies, standing at the stockade let its people out one at a time, and they run for home; each guard posted holds off two soldiers, and more soldiers work faster. Razing the camp still frees everyone at once

Gaps

  • The computer builds a camp and sends a pair of Slavers once Decadent, but they walk alone into the rival's town and mostly die; it never guards against them
  • A Normal or Hard computer now empties its tavern before it reaches Decadent, so it rarely gets there; an Easy one does not staff a tavern at all
  • How a human Decadent town actually fares (whether raiding keeps pace with its dying households) has not been played or measured
  • Night matters only to the work camp (escapes and risings); nothing else in the match changes with it
  • The computer posts no guards at its camp, so it loses captives every night, and now to a single soldier at the gate as well; the rule for posting them (postGuards and guardsShort in src/sim/rescue.ts) is written and tested but the computer does not call it yet
  • Escapes and risings are told in messages and by people appearing at the gate; no one is drawn climbing out or fighting
  • The captives' daily round has not been watched in play, only stills taken before they had moved
  • The computer never sends soldiers to get its people back, and does not answer a rescue at its own camp (heldAt in src/sim/rescue.ts says where they are held and the force needed, but nothing calls it yet)
  • A rescue is shown as a sign over the camp (a bar filling towards the next captive out, or soldiers against the number needed while the guards hold) and a line on its panel, but the soldiers themselves are not drawn doing anything at the gate, and the captives are not drawn coming out
  • Rescue was tried in tests only; how long it takes and how many soldiers a guard holds off are guesses, not played

Pagan units

First pass

A holy town cannot train them.

  • 3 units are marked pagan: the Shaman and the Slaver, as the owner settled it, and the Berserker, marked before that and left so
  • A town refuses them from the moment it is Devout; each says so in its description and on its locked button
  • The Prophet is not pagan: a faithful town and a worldly one may both call him
  • No nation's own unit is pagan, so none loses what is its own by keeping the faith

Gaps

  • Whether the Berserker should stay pagan has not been asked of the owner: only the Shaman and the Slaver were named
  • Marking the Slaver changes nothing in play: only a Decadent town could train him already, so a faithful one never could
  • Pagans already in a town when it turns to the faith stay and serve; nothing is done about them
  • Being pagan does nothing else: no effect on a town's standing for keeping them, and none in battle

Plague and calamity

Working, rough

Disasters that strike a town.

  • Plague and Earthquake, called down by a Prophet
  • Plague rising from a worldly town's Tavern
  • Fire from Heaven: a Prophet sets enemy buildings alight for 20 seconds
  • Fire spreads: any burning building (a Fire Lancer's work too) may set alight one standing within a tile and a half, friend or foe; what has caught once does not catch again for 45 seconds, walls do not burn, and rain stops it
  • Blight: a Prophet empties enemy farms for a minute and strips half the berries nearby
  • Weather tells on the harvest: farms yield 15% more in rain, 30% less in dust and 40% less in snow, and the farm's panel says so
  • The computer's Prophets use fire where buildings stand close and blight on a rival's fields
  • Lightning: while rain or a dust storm is heavy a bolt falls about every 45 seconds and two in five find a building, anyone's, towers, churches and wonders three times as often, never a wall; in rain the fire is short and does not spread, in dust it does
  • Famine: a food store bare for a minute leaves a town hungry, and after two it starves, losing one of its people every half minute (the old, then children, then soldiers who walk away, then grown villagers, never the last four); after four minutes plague breaks out round the Town Center, never among the Righteous; food in the store ends it at once; a war band without a hall and the armies of a field battle do not starve
  • Plague has a third source: famine
  • Fire spreads as the rule says: a seven-in-a-hundred chance each second for each building within reach (it had been all but settled once for each pair of buildings, so most never caught). Measured in a town of thirty-six houses a tile apart, one lit in a dust storm: left to itself 34 catch, none burns down and an eighth of the town's strength is lost in 41 seconds; with twelve villagers standing by 25 catch and under a fiftieth is lost
  • Fighting a fire: villagers right-clicked onto a burning building of their own beat it down before mending it, up to five to a fire, each taking a second and a half off it every second; idle villagers within nine tiles turn out unasked, and the panel counts who is at it

Gaps

  • No flood. Not attempted: the only water is the great lake of one kind of map, there are no rivers, and nothing in the map or the paths lets ground go under water and come back
  • Famine is tested as a rule only and its numbers are guesses; nobody eats day by day, so it comes only when nothing at all is coming in, and a trickle of food holds it off for ever
  • The computer player does nothing about hunger beyond what it already does for food: in three matches of twenty-five minutes its store was never bare for more than 31 seconds, but it has no rule for a famine if one came
  • Famine takes no account of the Market, of allies who could send food, or of captives, who do not go hungry
  • No fire by accident: nothing at the smithy or the hearth starts one
  • The computer player calls working villagers to a fire (turnOut in src/sim/calamity.ts) unless its bell is rung or raiders are in the town; tested for one fire in one town, not watched through a storm
  • Lightning and fire-fighting are tested as rules only: nobody has watched a storm over a town, and the numbers (how often it strikes, how fast hands put a fire out) are guesses
  • Lightning has no flash or thunder of its own: a strike shows as the usual fire and a message
  • No water is needed to fight a fire, and it does no harm to those fighting it
  • Fire in a crowded town is measured in a test (thirty-six houses a tile apart, eight matches), not watched: whether a whole town alight in forty seconds for an eighth of its strength is the right pace is undecided
  • Plague still cannot come from outside: no rats off a ship, no sickness carried by an army or a trader
  • A blighted field and a spreading fire have no look of their own beyond the usual flames and a warning on the panel

War 3

Land battle

Working

Train an army and fight with it.

  • 59 kinds of unit, with strengths against each other
  • Attack-move, patrol, stances, control groups, queued orders
  • Siege engines; units can shelter in Town Centers and towers
  • High ground: blows from above land a quarter harder, and archers reach farther
  • Mountains that cannot be crossed, with a pass always left open

Gaps

  • No formations
  • Unit strengths are untested guesses
  • The computer does not seek high ground or hold passes
  • Going uphill is no slower than going down

Navy

Working, rough

Ships on great-lake maps.

  • Docks, fishing boats, transports, Galleys and Frigates
  • Armies carried over water and landed

Gaps

  • One water map only: no islands, rivers or coastline
  • Two warships, the same for every nation

Allies

Working, rough

Fight alongside a friendly nation.

  • Teams in a skirmish; call your ally to a spot

Gaps

  • Sides are fixed at the start: no diplomacy during a battle

Nations and leaders 6

Nations

Working, rough

Thirteen peoples, each with bonuses and two units of their own.

  • 13 nations, each with two bonuses
  • Two unique units for every nation

Gaps

  • 14 unique units borrow another unit's figure with a plume and sash
  • Recording file names still use old internal ids (stonekin, dawn…)

People

Working, rough

Every person in the game is drawn from a real human figure with a skeleton and recorded motion, dressed and armed piece by piece.

  • A public-domain human figure (Quaternius, CC0) with 53 bones and 33 recorded motions
  • Faces are sculpted: every head is a modelled one from the Universal Base Characters by Quaternius (public domain), a man's and a woman's, with eyes, ears, brows in the colour of the hair, painted lips and a proper beard; its skin is multiplied by the person's own skin colour, so one head serves every people; hair is modelled too, in the person's own hair colour: a parted or a cropped cut for a man, long hair, twin buns or a crop for a woman, the cropped cut under a helmet, cap, fur hat, straw hat or turban so that it shows at the temples without standing up through, and none under a hood or coif (checked in pictures under every hat and helmet); no two people need look alike: there are five casts of the lower face for each sex (the jaw wider or narrower, the chin longer or shorter, the nose larger or broader) and five of the upper, chosen apart from it (eyes set wider or closer and larger or smaller, cheekbones broader or narrower, the ridge of the brow heavier or lighter, brows higher or lower, arched or level, heavy or fine), twenty-five faces a sex, five eye colours, and heads a little broader, narrower, longer or shorter; children have a child's cast of face: the paint softened away, a small jaw and chin, a button nose, full cheeks, larger eyes and fine brows; a helmet's nose guard is a slim bar to the bridge of the nose, so the face shows round it; the figure's own blank head is drawn in small, hidden inside. If the heads fail to load, the old simple faces are drawn instead
  • Drawn this way: villagers, children, all infantry, archers and gunners, priests and healers, heroes: 34 kinds of unit, each with its own dress and weapon
  • Men and women are built differently on the skeleton itself, and builds vary from person to person: slight, average or heavy, with a smaller or fuller chest
  • A fitted upper garment, skirts and cloaks that move with the body; hats, hoods, helmets, plate, mail, shields
  • A face fitted to the head: eyes, lids, brows, nose, lips, ears; hair, and beards on some men
  • Walking and running are played at the pace the unit really moves: slow units walk, quick ones run
  • A sword stroke for blades, a thrust for spears and pikes, a casting motion for holy men, each played through once per blow
  • Bows are drawn and guns and crossbows brought to the shoulder while there is someone to shoot at
  • People flinch when struck and fall with a recorded death
  • Villagers swing, kneel or stoop by trade with the right tool in hand, carry a sack or a bundle of logs on the back when loaded, and sometimes stand talking
  • Riders and drivers are the new figure too, seated astride the horses, camels, wolves, mammoths and elephants or standing in the chariots, each in their own dress with their own weapon
  • Horses are real animals with skeletons and gaits (Quaternius, CC0): they stand, walk and gallop under every rider and in front of every chariot, in six coats
  • Wolves, deer, stags and hounds are real animals too: they walk, run, bite, graze and fall
  • The camel, the war elephant, the mammoth and the war bear are real animals now as well: an elephant and a mammoth with trunk, tusks and great ears, a camel with its hump, a broad heavy bear
  • Children are small with a child's larger head
  • The crews of engines and ships, captives led on a rope and held in work camps, camp guards, the monks and nuns at a church and the maids and patrons at a tavern are the new figure too, each in their own dress
  • A woman's neckline, sleeves and head covering follow how pious or decadent her town is
  • Each nation dresses its villagers and its plain soldiers its own way (`src/client/dress.ts`): Romans bare-legged in short tunics with cloaks on their soldiers, Mongols in long-sleeved coats and fur hats, Franks in hoods and caps, Chinese in closed collars and straw hats with dark lacquered armour over long coats, Egyptians bare to the waist in linen kilts with a headcloth in their colour, Babylonians in long fringed robes and full beards, Persians in bright trousers and soft pale caps, Greeks bare-armed and bare-legged with bronze helmets, English in green and russet hoods with the kettle hat, Abbasids in long robes and turbans, Japanese in indigo leggings and straw hats with black or red lacquer, Arameans in long robes with a head-cloth or hood and a brown cloak, Norse in furs and dark wool; a nation's special units keep their own dress, and so do captives, monks, nuns and tavern folk
  • In a decadent town the tavern couple stand in each other's arms
  • The old figures built of blocks are gone from the game, which makes it about a tenth smaller to fetch; `?pitch=14` lowers the camera for a closer look

Gaps

  • The sculpted heads were seen large and face-on in pictures, in the game at its closest and usual zoom, and moving in runs of frames (standing, running, fighting); never at full frame rate and never on a real graphics card
  • Every face is still a reshaping of one man's or one woman's, so people differ but are plainly kin: the painting of the skin (the shadow of beard, the lined eyes) is the same on everyone of a sex
  • A cropped head of hair still shows a little through the crown of a straw hat on a woman's head; no woman in the game wears one, so it is seen only if one is ever given it
  • The edge of a woman's linen coif is ragged where it meets the brow
  • There are five heads of hair in all, so many people share one
  • A child's lips are still the woman's painted ones, a little too red
  • The camel, elephant, mammoth and bear are made over from the alpaca, the bull and the wolf, not modelled as themselves: close up the camel still looks like a llama
  • Riders have no riding motion of their own: they sit and strike
  • A knight's horse has lost its barding; only the saddle cloth is kept
  • Motion has been checked from short runs of frames, never watched at full speed
  • One recorded death; no shield block, no kneel to fire, no reload
  • Each nation's dress for its villagers and plain soldiers was seen standing in pictures, one man, one woman and three kinds of soldier for each of the thirteen, and the Abbasids standing in a game; no other nation in a game, and none moving (five nations put their soldiers in long coats, which have not been watched at a run), and not on riders or crews
  • Village women differ less from nation to nation than the men: their town still sets neckline, sleeves and head covering, and the cloth is their player's colour
  • Among the light soldiers the Romans, Franks, English and Greeks differ only in their legs and trim
  • Expressions are fixed; elbow joints of the base figure show faintly on bare arms
  • Cost with a hundred people on screen unmeasured

Land and wildlife

Working, rough

The ground of each country, what grows on it, and the animals that live there. Drawn only: none of it changes the rules.

  • Ground painted per country: green only near water in the desert, frosted grass and a shingle shore in the north, long dunes, snow drifts, flower meadows, dark leaf litter under trees, marsh pools
  • Five kinds of tree built from trunk, boughs and leaf clusters: oak, birch, fir with tiered boughs (snow-laden in the north), palm and acacia; two shapes of each, and the odd one turned gold
  • Which trees grow depends on the country: palms and acacias in the desert, fir and birch in the north
  • Bushes and berry bushes are thickets of leaf clusters; grass tufts are fuller
  • Bushes, boulders, reeds, cacti, fallen logs, mushrooms and lily pads, cleared away where you build or a road is laid
  • Grass and reeds sway in the wind
  • Flocks of birds with shadows, gulls over water, vultures over desert and steppe
  • Butterflies by day and fireflies by night in green country
  • Rabbits (white hares in the north) that bolt when a unit comes near
  • Fish leaping in open water, tumbleweed in dry country, pollen and dust in the air
  • Smoke from the Town Center and lived-in houses, thicker at night and in the cold

Gaps

  • Motion checked in still frames only
  • Trees do not sway
  • Leaves are faceted masses, not individual leaves or textures
  • Wildlife cost unmeasured on a phone
  • Fireflies are faint
  • No sounds for any of it

Play styles

Working, rough

Every nation belongs to a group that says how it plays, and every group has a rule of its own.

  • Each of the seven groups has one rule nobody else has, stated on the start screen with the nation; the numbers are `STYLE_RULES` in `src/sim/data.ts`
  • Builders: buildings mend themselves once the town has had fifteen seconds without an attack
  • Farmers: every finished farm makes room for one more villager, up to twelve
  • Scholars: every technology learned makes villagers gather 2% faster, up to 30%
  • The Faithful: their people are healed and cured of plague within six tiles of a Church
  • Traders: each Market earns gold by itself (up to three markets), and more for every other people that keeps one
  • Warriors: a soldier strikes 5% harder for each foe he has killed, up to 20%
  • Seven groups: raiders (Norse, Mongols), builders (Romans, Babylonians, Egyptians), farmers (Franks, English), scholars (Chinese, Greeks), the faithful (Arameans), traders (Persians, Abbasids), warriors (Japanese)
  • Raiders start as a war band: six fighters, a man and a woman, and no Town Center
  • They may raise a Town Center in the Stone Age, wherever they choose to settle
  • Any raider fighter on foot or horse carries off an enemy villager, who joins the tribe as a villager; men and women alike
  • Idle raiders take villagers alive on sight instead of killing them
  • A captive is freed if the raider leading them home is killed
  • A band with no buildings is beaten only when its last person falls

The map as a map

Working, rough

How the campaign map is drawn, and the weather and events that cross it.

  • Towns and camps hold up close to: stone and plaster are laid in courses with mortar, timber runs in planks, roofs are rows of overlapping tile, tent cloth has a weave; houses have windows (some lamp-lit), lintels and timber framing; a capital has a paved square with a roofed well, market stalls under coloured awnings, barrels, a cart and garden trees; a hamlet has a well, a woodpile and a fenced garden. `?look=cap` (or `foe`, `tribe`, a land number) opens the map close in on a place
  • Every world has its own make, named in the side panel: one great continent, several continents parted by straits, two lands with a sea between, lands round an inner sea, or an archipelago; and its own temper (cold, hot, parched, rainy, dry or mild), with more or fewer mountains and lakes
  • The world is a wide sea nearly twice the old size, and land rises only round the towns: fifteen to thirty of forty possible places are taken, so coasts, bays and island chains fall differently every time; lands parted by water are joined by the shortest crossing so none is cut off
  • Sea lanes: where open water lies between two lands the crossing is a sea lane, marked on the sea in dashes from shore to shore, faint until a Harbour stands at one end; an army makes the crossing only from a land with a Harbour, and lands at four-fifths strength; a capital always keeps one way out on foot; a computer player shut in by the sea builds a Harbour first
  • Painted like an illustrated map: strong, distinct colours for each kind of country (olive downs, deep woods, gold desert, red rock in the heat, blue-grey rock in the cold, heather, woods in autumn orange), every ridge drawn in gold light and violet shade, a dark blue-green sea clouded like paint with a bright turquoise band of shallows and creeping surf round every coast, a ragged shoreline with small isles standing off it, and soft-edged cloud shadow
  • A living country in three dimensions, in the manner of a great war-map: every campaign grows its own world from its seed, with its own coastline, bays, lakes, mountain ranges and rivers
  • Six kinds of country: green country, deep forest, marshland, steppe, desert and the frozen north. Which end of the world is cold, and where the dry lands lie, differ from world to world
  • The ground is painted by what grows there, with rock and snow on the heights and sand at the shore; fir stands in the cold, broadleaf in green country, palm in the heat, stone in the dry
  • A land's country is named when you click it, and a battle there is fought in that country: snow, desert, steppe or green
  • Worlds are larger: 15 to 30 lands, where there were 12 to 24
  • Borders glow in the colour of whoever holds the land; the chosen land is lit, lands within reach are tinted, marauder country is darkened
  • The ground is painted at twice the fineness of the map, with a grain over it for close looking: meadow among darker grass, red sand in the desert, strata down the rock faces, a ragged snow line, pale shallows and surf at the shore
  • A quilt of small ploughed fields and pasture lies round every town in green country; rivers have a dark bank and a thread of light
  • Slopes are lit warm on the sunny side and cool in shade, shadows are sharper, and the corners of the view fall away into shade
  • The land is modelled twice as finely as before and the high country is broken into crag and gully
  • Ground detail is worked out point by point as the picture is drawn, so it stays sharp however close you come: grain in the soil at four scales, and bare banded rock wherever the land is steep
  • Fields, rivers and roads are drawn at four times the fineness of the map where the graphics card allows
  • Trees are rounded and lumpy like foliage, with ragged fir skirts, and shaded smoothly; soft light comes from all round the sky; the sea is glossy and catches the sun
  • It moves: the sea runs, fair-weather cloud drifts over and shades the land, birds wheel, ships pass round the coast, smoke rises from every place that is lived in, banners fly
  • Armies march over the ground from where they stood to where they now stand
  • Mountains, rivers and open water bar a march: where one lies across the straight road between two towns, those lands are not neighbours, for armies, support and marauders alike
  • A dotted road joins every two towns an army can march between; where there is no road the way is barred, and clicking a land says by what
  • Passes and fords are left so the world is never cut in two, no land has a single way in, and no more than a quarter of borders close (about 13 a world)
  • Drag to move over it, wheel to come closer; the eye looks more across the land the closer it comes, and cloud thins so it never hides what is looked at
  • A capital is a walled city: a ring of battlemented wall with towers and a gatehouse, a keep roofed in its people's colour, and streets of houses inside. Fortified land has a castle; every other land has a hamlet
  • Each building raised on a land shows: a barn with haystacks and a fence for a farm, a hall behind a palisade for a barracks, a pier and boat for a harbour
  • Every people builds its own way: a Roman temple and tiled roofs, Mongol yurts round the khan's great tent with no wall, a Frankish castle with slate cones and a spired church, a Chinese pagoda behind grey walls and a red gate, pyramids, pylons and an obelisk in Egypt, the blue walls and ziggurat of Babylon, a Persian pillared hall with turquoise domes, a Greek temple on its rock, a Norman keep and parish church, an Abbasid mosque with minarets, a white Japanese castle with a torii, the Temple with its two bronze pillars for the Arameans, and a Norse mead hall behind a palisade
  • Houses, walls, towers, castles on fortified land and the buildings you raise all follow the people's style; banners in their colour hang on the gate
  • Land no people holds is built for its country: pitched tile and thatch in green country, flat roofs and domes in the desert, snow on the roofs in the north
  • Trees have shape and shade: firs in four tiers, broadleaf with full uneven crowns, slim birches, palms with drooping fronds, cactus in open desert, bushes, and tumbles of boulders
  • An army stands on a carved plinth like a piece on a war-table: a stone foot, a drum in its colour set with gilt studs, a gilt lip. Behind it is a field camp that grows with its numbers: striped pavilions, a rack of spears and shields, a fire with a pot, and crates and barrels for a large host

  • Marauders camp behind a ring of sharpened stakes, with dark tents, a cage, and skulls on poles

  • People stand in every tribal camp: one on watch at its edge, a woman crouched at the fire, a man talking across it; marauders stand in theirs, one with a torch
  • Armies are figures on a base in their colour under a standard, with their numbers on a shield; the figures are the same people as in battle, dressed for their age, with the Warlord among them when he is there

Day and night

Working, rough

The hours of the day, and what the dark changes.

  • A day is five minutes, the last three tenths of it night

  • There is a sky: a dome from the mist at the horizon up to blue or to the dark, the sun standing in it where the light comes from (gold when low, near white when high, with the sky brightest round it), a moon with its grey seas by night, and 650 stars that are the same every night; weather dims them
  • The main menu's camera now sits low and looks into the sunrise, so the sky and the morning sun are in the picture behind the town
  • By night the six flames nearest the middle of the view cast real light: walls, roofs, people and trees beside a lamp or a torch are lit on the side that faces it
  • The leader and about one soldier in three carry a torch by night: a flame at the shoulder and a glow on the ground at their feet

  • The sun crosses the sky from dawn to dusk: low and red at either end with long shadows, high and white at midday; by night a dim blue moonlight

Marauders

Working, rough

A band that belongs to no people: it carries off women, grows by them, and can be broken.

  • Four minutes into a skirmish or a siege, marauders make camp in the wilds, as far from every town as the land allows
  • They come out in parties to carry off women from the nearest people, one to a man, and lead them back to the camp
  • Every woman held bears the band children: the camp raises a new marauder about every two minutes for each woman it holds, up to 28
  • Finding the camp tells you how many of your women are held there; selecting it names them and how many children each has borne
  • Destroy the camp and everyone held goes free and comes home
  • A woman who was held there bears no more children, but works a quarter harder than the rest; one rescued on the road, before she reached the camp, is unharmed
  • On the campaign map marauders take root in a tribal land far from any capital, grow every turn, carry off women from the lands next to them (a supply less each turn, up to two, until they are freed), and overrun a neighbour when strong enough
  • Taking a marauder land breaks that camp: the women go home and the victor gains 4 supplies
  • Marauder land is washed black on the map and flies a black banner over a ring of stakes
  • `?marauders=0` leaves them out of a battle
  • A computer player learns where the camp is by losing a woman to it or by coming in sight of it, then marches on it once its army outweighs the band (sooner if its own women are held), and comes home when it is burnt
  • Its idle soldiers run down a raiding party that comes near its town: a marauder killed drops the woman he leads
  • They keep pace with the age: for each age the most advanced people has reached, a marauder has 30% more health and strikes 25% harder, and the camp's stockade is 30% stronger
  • A burnt camp is not the end: if any of the band are alive when it falls they make camp again five minutes later, on new ground at least 20 tiles from the ashes, and fresh men join them. Kill every last one first and the band is finished for the battle; every people is told which it is

  • The computer player hunts the camp but does not scout for it, and does not hide its women when a party comes; the rules for both (scoutSpots, partyNear and hideWomen in src/sim/marauders.ts) are written and tested but the computer does not call them yet
  • The computer comes home once the camp is burnt and does not hunt the survivors, so against computer players the band will usually come back
  • A band whose camp has been burnt stands where it is until the new camp is made: it does not flee or hide
  • Hunting was measured in one game: the camp fell at 16 minutes, not 30, and held 2 of that player's women at most, not 14
  • On the campaign map rival leaders ignore the marauders altogether
  • A player cannot run such a camp: marauders are always a third party
  • Strength by age and the return after a burning were tried in tests only: the numbers are guesses, and a late-age band has not been played against
  • A captive in the marauders' camp cannot be let out by soldiers at the gate as one in a Work Camp can: it must still be burnt
  • Tried in two games between computer players and in still pictures; never played against

Computer opponent

Working, rough

Rivals that build, fight and sail.

  • Builds a town, advances, attacks and defends
  • A people is beaten when its last Town Center has fallen and it has not begun another within a minute and a half (twenty seconds if it has no soldier left); a war band that has never settled is beaten as before
  • Goes for the Town Center once the defenders are down to their last two soldiers, and for one being raised at once; not before, which in trial games threw armies away
  • Builds a navy, raids by sea, lands armies
  • Staffs its Church and Tavern, hires mercenaries
  • Turns idle wealth into soldiers: the richer the treasury, the larger the army it keeps
  • Does not march into an army it cannot beat: it waits to outweigh the defenders, though not for ever
  • Calls a failed attack home instead of dying to the last man, and sends reinforcements up together, not one by one
  • In nine trial games between settled computer players every one ended with a winner, in 26 to 39 minutes (before, one in five ended inside 25)
  • Raiders: while the nearest rival has next to no soldiers the whole band goes for its villagers; later a few slip out at a time, keeping clear of towers and town centres, and a raider who is badly hurt comes home
  • A raider fights armed men before he takes villagers, and does not stand idle beside one he cannot take
  • Builds a market first when gold lies idle and food or wood has run out, and buys more at once the more gold it holds
  • Does not raise yesterday's soldiers once better ones can be trained, unless gold has run out
  • How many men make an army worth sending is set by the age, not the treasury: a rich player used to wait for an army it could never raise
  • Keeps no more than two priests or healers: pious peoples used to fill their armies with them
  • A long war makes it bolder: from the twenty-fifth minute it asks for a smaller margin, and after half an hour an attack is fought to the finish with every man sent up as he is raised
  • Builds more barracks, ranges and stables when its treasury is full, and sells a great heap of one thing faster

Gaps

  • Evenly matched wars are still long, and it is the middle of them that is long, not the end: in twenty-four trial games (twelve between settled peoples, twelve with a raider) the mean was 29.6 minutes and three ran past 35. Under the old rule the same games had a mean of 32.3, six past 35 and one with no winner at 45; twenty-two of the twenty-four were shorter, two longer, and two had a different winner. Both sets were run on the tree with lightning, fire-fighting and rival leaders' powers in it. Twenty-four games is a small sample
  • In those sixteen games raiders won 4 and settled peoples 8; sixteen games is too few to call that settled
  • The Norse were the weaker raiders; their food bonus now covers berries and fish as well as game, and in eight trial games they won 4, lost 3 and one was undecided (before: 1, 5 and 2). Eight games is a small sample
  • Towns still grow to 80 or 100 buildings; they no longer all have to fall, but that was never much of the length: under the old rule the loser's Town Center fell about two and a half minutes before the end
  • The computer player sends armies at a town in pieces through the long middle of a war and loses them; nothing was done about that
  • The clock on a people with no Town Center was seen in a browser running for the player and one rival together and counting down; the 20-second and 300-second cases, an ally's clock and the clock stopping when a hall is begun were not seen, and it is drawn from the same state the rule keeps
  • A computer player with wood and stone in store can begin Town Center after Town Center to stay in the war; in the trial games the loser was too poor to, and it has not been seen
  • A leader's temperament changes four things only: when it attacks, army size, walls, and church or tavern
  • Has never been played against by a person

Look and sound 6

3D world

Working, rough

The battlefield and its light.

  • Painted ground, scattered grass and stones
  • Shores run in smooth curves, not tile steps: the land falls away into the bed along a rounded line and the water thins to a soft edge of surf just short of it
  • Water takes its colour from the country: bright and clear in the desert, green over a temperate shelf, grey and cold in the north; rain rings its surface and the moon glints on it at night
  • Water with a moving surface of swell and chop: it mirrors the sky at a low angle and shows its depth looking down, catches the sun in points of glitter, shelves over several tiles from clear turquoise shallows with a net of light on the bed to deep blue, sends line after line of surf in to the beach, washes up and back along the sand, greys under weather and darkens at night
  • Hills that rise and fall and mountain ranges with snow on the peaks
  • Low warm sunlight, soft shadows, haze and drifting cloud shadows
  • A drawn finish: banded shade with an ink outline, heavy on buildings and fine on people
  • Weather that comes and goes: rain, snowfall in the north, dust in the desert

Gaps

  • The computer opponent does not plan around the weather
  • No seasons, no thunder, no sound of rain
  • Water mirrors a sky with drifting cloud but not the land or ships; ships ride the long swell but not the small chop
  • The water costs more to draw than anything else on the ground and has not been measured on a phone
  • Rain rings on water and ships riding the swell run but have not been seen
  • How fast it runs on a phone or in a big battle has not been measured

Buildings

Working, rough

What a town is made of.

  • Every building shaped by hand, with a ruined church for a decadent town: pitched roofs with eaves, timber framing, tiles and thatch, stonework, round towers, props
  • A building going up rises from its footings inside a scaffold of poles; roofs differ a shade from house to house
  • Walls join up: each piece reaches toward its neighbours, corners and ends get a heavier post, gates turn to stand across the wall
  • Each nation keeps its own roofs (domes, pagodas, flat roofs, tents) and colours by age
  • Church and tavern drawn bright, plain, ruined or gaudy by the town's standing
  • Each nation's people look like where they come from: fair in the north and west of Europe, olive round the Mediterranean, tan to brown in the Near East and on the Nile, light tan with black hair on the steppe and in the Far East; within a people the shades are close, and a captive keeps the look of the people they were taken from
  • People differ from one another: hair worn short, long, knotted or balding, beards and moustaches on some men, and faces with brows, a nose, a mouth and ears
  • What flies has its own shape and ending: fletched arrows and bolts point first, stones and boulders tumbling, cannon balls nearly flat; they land in dust, a grey puff or a splash, and a boulder shakes the ground
  • An engine or ship below half strength burns and smokes, worse as she nears her end; a troop ship shows up to six of those aboard
  • A ship that is sunk breaks up: masts, yards and sails come down around her, her crew go into the water, and the hull rolls over and settles by the stern in foam; a wrecked engine throws its crew clear
  • Siege engines are built as machines with their working parts and a crew: a catapult with its skein, stop bar and winch; a ram hung on chains under a roof of hides; a bolt-thrower with spring frame and slide; a trebuchet with trestles, counterweight box and sling; a banded great gun on a field carriage; a two-tier organ gun behind a mantlet
  • Four ships: a fishing boat with a lateen sail, net and catch; a galley with a ram, shields along the rail and five oars a side; a troop cog with castles and cargo; a warship with bowsprit, two masts with topsails and guns run out
  • Clothes have collars, cuffs, stitched hems, belt buckles and lacing; boots have soles and turned cuffs; hands have thumbs
  • Village women dress as their town lives: scarves where it is pious, laced bodices worn low where it is decadent, lower still at the extreme
  • In a worldly or decadent town a tavern with both maids and patrons almost never has a child (tens of minutes apart at most), and then one no household owns to; hearts rise from its busy tavern and from houses where a couple is at home, and a maid and a patron are found in each other's arms round the side
  • A day is five minutes, its last three tenths night: the light goes blue and dim and the top bar says so
  • The work camp is a four-tile compound of two rooms: a workroom where the women are kept at the querns by day, and a bunkhouse the men go out from to break stone and back into; at dusk all are shut into the bunkhouse and a lamp shows
  • Slavers can be posted at a camp as guards (up to four); each makes its men count a quarter more toward the town's work
  • Holding people has a cost: each guard watches three captives; by night an unwatched one slips away about once a night and runs for home, and a camp with four or more men unwatched may rise, burn and lose half of all it holds at a stroke
  • A Decadent town is dying out and grows by what it takes: its households have children less than half as often and lose half of them, villagers take twice as long to raise at the Town Center, and its Work Camp holds twelve
  • A villager taken by a Slaver is seen: stripped to a loincloth (a woman keeps a band across the chest), barefoot, roped at the neck and wrists, led behind the Slaver and then held in the work camp yard, the men at labour
  • A decadent town has women's clothes left about its tavern and two houses in three: a dress in a heap, a shift, shoes, and one hung by the door; a pious town keeps flowers at its doors

Gaps

  • Walls have not been checked on sloping ground or beside towers
  • One wonder for every nation, differing only in its roof
  • Houses come in four shapes with varied yards (woodpile, barrel, vegetable patch, washing line, well); the storehouse and barracks have two; the steppe keeps its houses, town centre, church, tavern, storehouse, barracks, smithy, stable, range and workshop under round felt tents (its market, tower and dock are still walled); every other building still has one shape
  • Nations share one set of building shapes under their own roofs

Damage and death

Working, rough

What fighting does to people and buildings.

  • Buildings shed pieces as they weaken and fall apart when destroyed
  • Blows draw blood that spatters the ground and fades
  • Wounds show on the figure in two stages, at two thirds and one third strength, and again in death: cuts and blood painted on the body and clothes
  • Soldiers lose a shield or a helmet first, then the rest of their armour; the pieces fall to the ground and hair shows where a helmet was
  • Villagers' clothes tear to show skin, and a hat is knocked off; never over the chest or the loins
  • Riders and crews are marked the same way; healing puts gear and clothes back and takes the dropped pieces off the ground
  • The dead do not lie alike: they fall to either side, at different speeds and angles, and end in one of three sprawls
  • People flinch when struck and fall with a recorded death; animals fall with their own; engines fly apart, ships sink
  • Blood and torn clothes can be switched off

  • Tears and cuts are painted patches, coarse on skirts and cloaks; no real rips
  • Animals show no wounds
  • Riders do not fall from their horses
  • One recorded fall underneath: the dead differ in which way they lie and how they sprawl, not in how they go down
  • The switch for blood is an address option, not a setting in the menu
