Town and economy 5

Gathering

Working

Villagers bring in food, wood, gold and stone.

  • Berries, farms, hunting and fishing for food
  • Trees, gold and stone to work
  • Storehouses as drop-off points

Gaps

  • Every rate and cost is a guess: nobody has played it for balance

Building and walls

Working

Raise a town and fortify it.

  • 30 buildings across two build pages
  • Palisades, stone walls, gates and towers; drag to draw walls
  • A Wonder that wins the game if it stands

Gaps

  • Buildings need nearly level ground, but nothing tells you why a spot is refused

Ages and research

Working

Advance through five ages and buy upgrades.

  • 5 ages from Stone to Gunpowder
  • 52 upgrades: tools, weapons, armour, unit lines
  • In a campaign battle nobody ages up: a town is in its nation's age, which is advanced on the map (the button says so, and the computer does not save for it). Skirmishes and missions age up as before

Gaps

  • The research list is short and the same for every nation

Roads

Working, rough

Every building is joined to its town centre.

  • A road is laid the moment a building is placed, and joins nearby roads into streets
  • Units move faster on roads and their routes prefer them
  • Dirt tracks become cobbles from the Iron Age
  • Saved with the game

Gaps

  • You cannot lay or remove a road yourself
  • Roads do not join one town to another
  • Only checked on level ground

Market

Working, rough

Buy and sell resources for gold.

  • Buying and selling at the Market
  • For the Traders (Persians, Abbasids) a Market earns gold by itself, more for every other people that keeps one

Gaps

  • No trade routes or caravans to be seen or raided: the Traders' markets earn by rule, with nothing on the road
  • No trade between players beyond gifts to an ally

People and family 5

Families

Working

Men and women marry into houses and raise children.

  • A man and a woman in a house raise villagers for half the food
  • Children are never shown and never on the map (`src/sim/nursery.ts`): a child is an entry on the record of the house it is born to (name, family, what it takes after its parents in), counted among the town's people from birth, and at coming of age a grown villager steps out of the door as that person and takes up a parent's trade; the house's panel lists its children in words, with their age and the years until they are of age
  • Nothing on the map can reach a child: no blow, raid, capture, famine or fire. When a house falls its children pass unharmed to the nearest house of their town, or its Town Center, or any roof it has; a people with no roof keeps them with it; the death or capture of the parents does not touch them
  • A child no household owns to, born at a tavern, is kept at the Town Center
  • No child ever dies. Where a town's creed makes families weak, a share of the births that are due do not come (1 in 50 among the Righteous, 1 in 10 in an ordinary town, half in a Decadent one), so fewer grow up; the house keeps a count of them and shows it on its panel, and nothing else is said
  • Family names, renown for large families, four villager traits
  • Villagers grow old and die in a skirmish or a mission; in the campaign a person ages only on the map, a season at a time, and each person's age is kept on the town's record (`src/sim/ageing.ts`): a child is grown at fifteen, the grown are old at sixty, and the old die over the years after (0.8 in a hundred a season at sixty, 0.4 more each year past it, set a little low as war, plague and hunger take some of the old too: of those who reach sixty, about four in five are gone by seventy). In a battle or visit nobody grows older, children do not grow, and nobody dies of age

Gaps

  • Inside a town nothing happens to a widow, a widower or an orphan; on the map they are kept (widowhood.ts): a husband fallen with the army leaves a widow, a notable mother who dies a widower; after a year's mourning they may marry again in the town or go to marry in another town of their people, and a widow who dies leaves her children to a named house of the town
  • A year of a child's age is a fifteenth of the 25 seconds it takes to grow, not a year of the campaign's seasons
  • Children taken in from a fallen house keep their own family name but nothing ties them to the house that took them in
  • The house panel's list of children has not been seen in a browser
  • The computer player is unchanged and needs no change: it only ever asked whether a villager was grown, and every villager now is

A soldier costs a person

Working, rough

Training a soldier takes a villager as well as the price.

  • Step 1 of `docs/DESIGN.md`; the rule is in `src/sim/muster.ts`. Every soldier, engine crew, ship (fishing boats too), Slaver and healer trained at a building takes one villager: he walks to the building and goes in, and only then does the training time run and turn him into the unit, under his own name and with his own years
  • Who goes is chosen for the player: a man with no house before a husband, then the nearest man with nothing to do, and if nobody is idle the nearest man at work. A husband is sent only when no other man is left, so a household is not broken while there is anyone else to send. Soldiers, crews and Slavers come from the men only. A healer (Shaman, Priest, Prophet, Herbwife) may be a woman, and a woman is asked first so the men are left for the ranks; she is a woman still as a healer
  • With nobody free the order is refused, nothing is paid, and the player is told ("No man is free to be trained as a ...")
  • Cancel the order, or lose the building, and he comes back out as he was; cancelled on the road, he stops. If he dies on the way or is sent elsewhere, the building calls the next man
  • Cost nobody: a villager (born to housed couples, and in a skirmish or mission still trained at the Town Center), the beasts (hound, mammoth, bear, elephant) and the nation's leader. Units that do not come out of a building arrive as before: a war band's first seven fighters, marauders, hired swords, and the army brought to a campaign battle
  • A woman who fights strikes at two fifths of a man's strength (hunting is not fighting)
  • The Train button says it takes a man (or a villager, for a healer)
  • Tested in `tests/muster.test.ts`

Gaps

  • In a skirmish or mission it still bites less than the design means: a villager is bought at the Town Center for 50 food in 16 seconds, so a town buys back the man it sent (in forty-eight trial wars the largest army fell from 27.0 to 22.9 and the wars were hardly longer). In the campaign it no longer can: see People come only by birth
  • The player cannot pick who goes, and nothing on screen shows a man on his way or says that an order is waiting for one
  • A soldier now grows old and dies as the villager he was would have: seen in the rules, never watched in play
  • A husband sent to arms (only when no unmarried man is left) leaves his house without a man: the computer moves another man in, but nothing does so for the player, and he is not counted to his house as the design asks
  • Never played by hand

People come only by birth

Working, rough

In the campaign no villager is bought: a town has the people it was loaded with and those its couples raise.

  • `src/sim/birthonly.ts`. In a campaign battle or a visit (`Game.bornOnly`, set where `Game.fixedAge` is and kept with a saved battle) the Town Center trains no villager: the order is refused, nothing is paid, and the button is locked and says why. A skirmish or a mission hires villagers as before
  • The computer lives under it: it does not ask for villagers, it builds a house for every man and woman with no roof (two going up at a time), and it sends an unmarried man to arms before a husband (muster.ts). Its floor of working villagers before it sends anyone to arms is unchanged
  • Measured in forty-eight campaign-shaped trial sieges, computer against computer (`scripts/sieges.test.ts`: a real campaign's battle over a town, with the town's own people and stores and marauders in the wilds, cut off at 30 minutes; the rival against the capital on even seeds, the capital's army against a tribe's town on odd), run with villagers for hire and without on the same seeds, when births were still at the skirmish pace (the slower pace is in the gaps below). Undecided at 30 minutes: 20 with hire, 13 without. Mean length (undecided as 30): 23.3 to 21.3 minutes; decided ones 18.5 to 18.0. Twenty-five were shorter, eighteen longer, and the winner changed in twenty-three. In the eighteen still going at the 20th minute both ways, a defending town had 16.2 villagers and 11.2 soldiers with hire and 8.8 and 10.8 without; the attacker's camp 12.6 and 8.8, then 10.0 and 11.9. Soldiers held up and villagers halved. A besieged town held 19 times of 24, up from 13. None of it was tuned
  • Tested in `tests/birthonly.test.ts`

Gaps

  • A side can die out and not be beaten: with no villager and no child left it can never get another, and its soldiers and hall hold the battle open. In the trial sieges six of the thirteen undecided were like that (one of twenty with hire), and fourteen sides died out more than a minute before the end
  • Births in a battle or a visit are slowed to a pace the owner chose (birthonly.ts): a housed couple's first child takes about fifteen minutes, half again a visit (later ones longer, as in any house), and what is born in a visit counts against the season's births on the map, and a child takes sixty visits' worth of time to come of age, so none born there grows up there. With that pace the trial sieges were shorter and the attacker's camp could not raise an army: 7 of 48 undecided at 30 minutes (13 with fast births, 20 with hire), mean 16.1 minutes; a besieged town held 23 times of 24; at the 20th minute of the seven still going, a side had 1.1 villagers; per siege a defending town trained 8.8 and the attacking camp none (it never has more than the eight working hands the computer keeps before sending one to arms), and 1.6 and 1.4 were born. With nobody growing old in the battle, much the same: 6 undecided, mean 16.7, the camp trained 0.9. Skirmish keeps the old pace
  • The attacker's camp in a siege still starts with four villagers from nowhere (a war band with a man and a woman), and breeds like a town: 26 births a siege against the defending town's 22
  • A town with nobody in it stays empty all battle, and a town of women only (or men only) never grows: both seen in trial runs. On the map a town taken empty gets four of the army's followers (township.ts); a town emptied while held does not
  • A raiding band that carries off a small town's people early leaves it nothing to rebuild from: against four founders the town was empty by the fifth minute in three games of three, and the band, settled and breeding from its captives, won in 14 to 16 minutes
  • Nothing tells the player, beyond the locked button, that their people are now all they have; never played by hand

Gaps

The leader on the field

Working, rough

Your nation's leader fights in person: made in a creator, hardened by battle, shaped by the town's piety.

  • A hero creator, opened from the menu and from the campaign map: name, man or woman, calling (Warlord or Strategist), six points across Might, Guard and Vigour, and a turning figure to dress: hair, cloak, headgear, build, beard
  • One leader is kept for each nation on this device and leads it in every skirmish and campaign
  • The first hero of their calling raised in a battle is the leader, by name; the other calling is a plain lieutenant
  • Veterancy: enemies felled within the leader's reach are their deeds, and deeds raise them through five ranks, Blooded to Legend
  • The creator also sets the leader's skin (eight shades from fair to dark, or left to their people) and face (eight that differ in both halves, or left to chance); both are kept with the hero and worn in battle and on the campaign map
  • Each rank adds health, and strength or armour as chosen in the creator: all to the arm, all to armour, or the two by turns
  • They are still flesh: however armoured, a leader takes at least three tenths of every blow, and one who falls is raised again a rank lower
  • Starting a campaign asks for your people and who leads them: the nation's leader as written, that leader's other self, or one of your own making
  • Every leader keeps a house: one child in their teens at the start (of age in two years), another every five turns while the leader lives, up to three; an heir comes of age at fifteen years (60 seasons), as everyone does
  • When your leader falls you are asked who follows: an heir of age keeps the parent's gifts and the house, and begins with half their veterancy; or a captain from an army that has won a battle takes command where they stand with that army's veterancy, and the old house ends
  • You may decide later, or hire a stranger at the capital as before; the stranger is a new person and ends the house
  • Rival peoples settle their own succession the same way: eldest heir of age, else their best captain, else a hired stranger
  • Births and successions are written in the chronicle, and the capital's panel names the leader and the heirs
  • In a battle the dead stay dead: the one raised after the leader falls is their heir by name (the next number), a rank lower
  • The call: from Seasoned a leader has one power to set off by hand, from their panel, once every ninety seconds. A Warlord sounds the charge and everyone of theirs within reach strikes a third faster for twelve seconds; a Strategist bids them hold fast and they take 30% less harm
  • Played through by the game's own commands in tests/leader-play.test.ts: a town advances an age and trains its leader; trained spearmen are sent against a work party and one makes his name with four kills while the leader, standing by, becomes Blooded; the leader is ridden down by knights; the named spearman is raised and leads with a Blooded Warlord's battle cry
  • Also played there: a campaign siege fought to its end by computer players on both sides, whose leader, survivors and best soldier (a crossbowman, nine kills) are handed home to the map; and a leader lost on the march at turn 2, followed by their son at turn 5
  • Soldiers make a name: every soldier's kills are counted, and one on foot or horse who has felled three is known by name from then on
  • When your leader has fallen in a battle, such a soldier can be raised on the shields from their panel: they become the leader where they stand with a rank for every three felled, and the old line ends
  • One raised from the ranks goes on fighting as what they were: a knight leads from the saddle, an archer from behind the line, with a leader's presence about them and the Warlord's powers to choose from. They take better arms when their kind does, and in a campaign their army brings them to the next battle the same way
  • One soldier in four who makes a name is a woman, named and addressed as one when raised
  • In a campaign the soldier who felled most and lived is remembered with the army, and is the captain a succession offers, by name and deeds; a soldier raised in the battle itself leads on the map afterwards
  • A leader who fell in a campaign battle is counted as fallen even if another hero was raised later in it
  • Powers: at Blooded, Veteran and Legend a leader comes into one of two powers for their calling. Each can be chosen in the creator or left open; one left open is chosen on the field when its rank is earned, from two buttons on the leader's panel, and cannot be changed after. What was chosen in a campaign battle goes home with the leader, and in a skirmish is kept with them. A Warlord's are a stronger battle cry or a longer reach; health from every blow or a shield against shot; dread that weakens enemies or a fury that quickens as they are wounded. A Strategist has six of their own
  • A power changes the leader on the field the moment the rank is reached, is named in the message and listed on their panel, and survives a saved game
  • A skirmish leader keeps the rank they finish a battle with, and brings it and its powers to the next; if they fell, it is their heir who is kept
  • Each rival people's leader is built to suit how it lives (raiders all arm, builders all armour, and so on) with powers of its own, in skirmish and campaign alike; six peoples have a making of their own, so no two who live alike are led alike, and Babylon's and Greece's leaders are Strategists
  • On the campaign map a leader adds half a point of strength per rank to the army they march with, in attack, in defence, in what the panel shows and in what rivals reckon before they march
  • Whoever leads a people now is named wherever the leader is: beside their speeches, in the list of peoples, in demands, truces and alliances, and beside your own leader's words in battle; a rival's succession is told in the log and the chronicle
  • A holy town's leader lends 1 armour to everyone of theirs nearby, 2 if the town is Righteous
  • A decadent town's leader binds enemy villagers in half a Slaver's time and leads them to a Work Camp; while the leader is in the field the camp's women bear two and a half times as fast (their children are records and raise the camp's yield; they are no longer villagers for the keeper)
  • One person in one place (src/sim/court.ts): on the campaign map the leader is in one land of their people, in its town or at the head of the army that stands there. They ride with that army when it marches unless told to keep to the town, and can travel alone to a neighbouring land of their own once a turn
  • A battle has the leader in it only if it is fought where they are: with the army they ride with, or in the town attacked, garrison or none. Anywhere else the army or town fights without them: no leader unit, nobody trained there becomes the leader, so no powers and no call
  • The leader steadies the land they are in: no unrest begins there, and unrest already there passes when it comes to a head, though no army stands in it
  • The armies can be called to the leader: every army marches a land nearer each turn through their own country until all have come or the call is ended, and joins the army with the leader on arrival; from then they march as one
  • A leader whose town is taken with no army about them gets away to a neighbouring land of their people, the capital for choice, or is killed where none adjoins
  • An heir takes up the rule in the capital and needs no army there; a captain or a soldier raised in battle leads from where their army stands
  • Rivals live by the same rule and keep it simply: a rival's leader rides with the army they are with and never leaves it; one who takes up the rule does so in the capital and joins the first army raised or arriving there. Rivals do not call their armies or keep to a town
  • In the campaign the leader's rank is kept from battle to battle, rises with a battle won, and drops one when they fall
  • Rival nations' leaders take the field too, as written, with their points spread evenly
  • The leader's panel shows rank, deeds to the next, and what the town gives them

Gaps

  • Two callings only
  • The leader's place on the map is checked by tests only: the buttons on the land panel (bring the leader here, call the armies, keep to the town) and the crown over an ungarrisoned town have not been seen in a browser, and the turning map draws no figure for a leader without an army
  • Steadying a town is one effect only, on unrest; nothing yet for faith, output or the town's people, which wait on towns that persist
  • Calling the armies calls all of them, strips every garrison on the way, and will not cross land that is not their own or wait for a full army to make room
  • A battle settled on the map does not count a leader standing in an ungarrisoned town, and a rival attacked in a town its leader holds alone is settled without them
  • A leader travels one land a turn like an army, by road or sea lane, with no cost and no risk on the way
  • Choosing a power on the field is checked by tests only: its two buttons have not been seen in a browser, and nothing but a line in the messages and on the panel tells the player a choice is waiting
  • A rival always has its powers chosen beforehand; a leader of yours fought by the computer (a reserve) takes the first of each pair
  • One power only is set off by hand, the call; a rival's leader raises theirs the moment they are struck at, with no thought for when it would tell most
  • Veterancy was slowed after a played siege took a leader from Untried to Legend in nineteen minutes: Veteran, Renowned and Legend now ask twice the deeds (41, 77 and 125 in all). The new pace has not been played by a person, and a leader who had a rank saved under the old count keeps it
  • Every number here is a first guess: points, ranks, the armour lent, the camp's rate
  • The leader speaks no lines of their own on the field
  • Rival leaders never take captives when decadent and are not kept out of danger: the computer marches them with the army like any other soldier
  • A Strategist's people (Babylon, Greece) have no leader on the field until the computer raises a Strategist, an age later than a Warlord; whether that weakens them has not been measured
  • A skirmish leader's rank is kept only when the battle is fought to its end, and only on this device; nothing stops it climbing to Legend against Easy rivals
  • Heirs have no mother, no marriages and no rival claims: a house is a list of children and nothing more
  • A new leader speaks the old one's lines, so a successor may still give the dead leader's name in their own words; portraits and recorded voices are the old leader's too
  • Only the one best soldier of a battle is remembered with the army, and only until its next battle or until it joins another army; a battle settled on the map remembers nobody
  • A soldier raised to lead has the same making and a Warlord's powers whatever they fought as; a woman among the soldiers is drawn like the men until she is raised; a leader on horseback or with a bow in the leader's cloak has not been looked at
  • A skirmish forgets a soldier raised in it: the leader you made is kept instead
  • Rival peoples never raise a soldier in battle: when their leader falls there they wait for the map
  • Raising a soldier is reached by the game's own commands in a test (see has), and its button was seen once in a browser with a soldier set up by hand; no person has done it
  • Succession is reached by campaign commands in a test (see has) and its dialog was seen once in a browser on an edited save; no person has played to one
  • Powers count for nothing when a battle is settled on the map: only the rank does
  • The call, the powers, rival builds and kept skirmish rank are checked by tests and one look at the creator; none has been used by a person
  • Face, skin and clothing colours other than the cloak cannot be chosen
  • The creator has been seen in pictures on a slow test renderer, not used by a person
  • Skin and face in the creator: a dark skin and one face were seen applied to the turning figure, from the side; the eight faces were not compared with one another there, and the chosen look was not seen on the leader in a battle or on the campaign map

Faith and vice 9

Piety and decadence

Working, rough

A town drifts between Righteous and Decadent, with boons and banes each way.

  • Five standings, each changing births, plague, gold, work and more
  • A town looks its standing: church bright or in ruins, tavern plain or hung with lanterns, people and houses drawn modest or gaudy
  • 9 dilemmas that ask you to choose
  • Each nation starts with its own leaning

Gaps

  • Only 9 dilemmas, so they repeat
  • The numbers behind every boon and bane are guesses
  • Which way each nation leans is my guess, not yours

Church

Working, rough

Villagers serve as monks and nuns; the town grows pious.

  • Up to four clergy per Church
  • More children, shorter plagues, some plagues prevented
  • A holy town trains Priests and refuses pagan units

Gaps

  • Monks and nuns do the same thing: no difference between men and women here, unlike the Tavern
  • No feast days
  • A Church near a Tavern has no effect on it

Drift

Working, rough

A strained town's people turn to what is worse for them; a well-kept town has them back.

  • On the map (`src/sim/drift.ts`), once a season: strain is read from what the game already keeps (hunger from the town's own larder, larder.ts, or a famine on its land; raids; sickness; unrest; the first two seasons after it was taken by force, conquered.ts; grief, grief.ts, and the loss of known people by their fame, notableweight.ts). A town hungry or raided, or newly taken, sends one or two a season, women to the tavern if the town has one and men to the mines, by turns, up to half of each; they are counted on the town's record. A mild strain alone (a short larder, a sickness, a grief) sends nobody and lets two a season come back; with care and no strain, two or three. The town's own standing (townfaith.ts) falls with strain (a newly taken town's unrest excepted: its yoke weighs on it already) and a little more for every two women at the tavern; its nation's, being its towns', follows
  • What the drifted do: gold from the mines and the tavern every season; men at the mines leave the walls thin (threats.ts reads the share still at their posts, `watchOf`, for a town's men and walls); a woman at the tavern is no wife at home (seasonfolk.ts); and they eat where they work (larder.ts reads the counts)
  • A nation that has lately lost known people dips in standing, all its towns together, by notableweight's `nationLoss`
  • Inside a campaign battle or a visit (`src/sim/driftintown.ts`): the town turns out with its drifted women at the tavern and its drifted men at the gold; strain (hunger, a raid, weighing up to half as much again in a town whose homes do not steady it, grieving houses, sickness) sends more the same way, one at a time, about one in forty seconds at full strain; care brings a tavern woman or a miner back, or sends someone to the church; each is said. A drifted villager is marked, and the computer does not set them back to its own work. When the battle or visit ends, those marked at the tavern and the mine are the town's drifted. A skirmish has none of it
  • The land's panel says how the town stands, which way it is drifting, who of it has drifted, and whether it grieves
  • Measured over twenty seasons in eight computer-run campaigns, towns eating (`scripts/drift.test.ts`, untuned): with no decrees, 8 to 10% of the nations' towns had anyone drifted at a time, about 0.1 a town, and nine in ten of their men at their posts; their standing moved +4.9 on the mean, 24% fallen more than ten and 34% risen more than ten by the twentieth season; the tribes' towns fell -6.1 (33%). In forty-eight trial sieges with drift in the town: 7 undecided at 30 minutes, mean 16.1 minutes, besieged towns held 24 of 24
  • Tested in `tests/drift.test.ts` and `tests/driftintown.test.ts`

Gaps

  • The rates (one or two a season, one drift in forty seconds in a town, eight points a season at full strain) are tuned to the health report, not played
  • Only the tavern and the mines: nobody yet drifts to anything else worse for them

Decrees

Working, rough

The leader rules a town by decree as well as by building.

  • `src/sim/decrees.ts`. Six decrees, each given to one town, paid in supplies (food and tending excepted) and holding for some seasons: call them to church (needs a church; draws the town toward the holy way), post guards (what strains it tells half as much, but nothing draws it up meanwhile), buy grain abroad (src/sim/grain.ts: new food from outside the nation, paid in its gold alone, the hungry town's first: the town's larder brought up to two seasons' eating, at 1 gold a food, 1.5 in a famine year, a first guess agreed with the food session; by messenger half comes and half is paid; the computer buys only when the nation can spare the gold, plenty.ts at PLENTY.grain, and beside the town's one decree of the season rather than instead of it; it replaced food sent between the nation's own towns, which only moved a lean season from one larder to the next; a sensible ruler sends it to two towns a season at most, and gives no tending where a Monastery or Infirmary tends already), close the tavern (no drift to it, no gold from it, and a decadent town is angered), order the women into the tavern (gold every season, standing falls), and have the church tend the sick and the mourning (needs a church, costs the town 40 gold: sickness and grief weigh half as much there at full strength, and plague kills a quarter fewer)
  • Given in person where the leader is (court.ts); anywhere else it goes by messenger, half as strong
  • A button for each on the land's panel, with its cost and why it cannot be given
  • The computer's peoples give the decrees a sensible ruler would each season (food where hungry, guards where raided or rising, church or a shut tavern where sliding), keeping four supplies in hand
  • Felt inside a battle or visit too: guards slow the drift there (by half at full strength), a shut tavern keeps its women at their work, the women ordered into the tavern stay and are not drawn back, a call to church draws people to it the sooner; food sent is already in the town's stores
  • On the map a shut tavern sends its drifted women home, and the women ordered into it are half of them, kept there
  • A decree given in the player's part of a season holds through the turn that follows, so it acts in that season's drift as a rival's does
  • Measured over twenty years in 96 computer-run campaigns (scripts/health.test.ts) by standing lost to drift alone, every people giving sensible decrees: 27.7 a town with decrees, 31.8 with none (13.0% less; 10.8% before the church tended the sick). About two fifths of drift's toll is sickness, unrest and grief, and most decrees go by messenger at half strength, after the strain
  • Tested in `tests/decrees.test.ts`

Gaps

  • The costs, strengths and lengths are guesses
  • Nothing yet stops the player giving every decree to every town they can pay for

Grief

Working, rough

A home that loses its mother or its sons works more slowly for a time.

  • `src/sim/grief.ts`. In a town, when the woman of a house who has raised a child dies or is carried off, or a son born under its roof dies (at home or at war), the house grieves for three minutes and its people gather, build and fight up to two fifths slower; a man gone to be trained is not mourned
  • It tells most in a town of middling morals and half as much at the holy or the decadent end
  • A grieving house's people wear the grieving mark (portrait.ts)
  • On the map a bereaved town grieves for four seasons, as long as the loss of a known person is felt (notableweight.ts), which strains it (drift.ts), most for the famous
  • Tested in `tests/grief.test.ts`

Gaps

Slavers and work camps

Plague and calamity

Working, rough

Disasters that strike a town.

  • Plague and Earthquake, called down by a Prophet
  • Plague rising from a worldly town's Tavern
  • Fire from Heaven: a Prophet sets enemy buildings alight for 20 seconds
  • Fire spreads: any burning building (a Fire Lancer's work too) may set alight one standing within a tile and a half, friend or foe; what has caught once does not catch again for 45 seconds, walls do not burn, and rain stops it
  • Blight: a Prophet empties enemy farms for a minute and strips half the berries nearby
  • Weather tells on the harvest: farms yield 15% more in rain, 30% less in dust and 40% less in snow, and the farm's panel says so
  • The computer's Prophets use fire where buildings stand close and blight on a rival's fields
  • Lightning: while rain or a dust storm is heavy a bolt falls about every 45 seconds and two in five find a building, anyone's, towers, churches and wonders three times as often, never a wall; in rain the fire is short and does not spread, in dust it does
  • Plague crosses between peoples: anyone within a tile and a third of another people's sick may catch it, twelve chances in a hundred each second for an ordinary town, more for the worldly, a third as readily the Righteous; so a sick army brings it into the town it attacks
  • Plague aboard ship: in a transport's hold one sick passenger gives it to the rest
  • Plague with trade: one purchase in a hundred at a Market brings plague round it, never twice in three minutes
  • Fire by accident: a Blacksmith at work may catch from its forge, and by night a lived-in house from its hearth, a hamlet less often than a town; never in heavy rain, twice as often in a dust storm
  • A hungry town's warning says what it can do about it, and its allies are told and can send food from a Market
  • Acts of God go by creed (docs/DESIGN.md): one measure, a third for the Righteous, a half for the Devout, a quarter more for the Worldly, half again for the Decadent, is applied to how often and how hard plague, fire and famine fall; nobody is immune any longer, the Righteous included
  • On the campaign map, a season of acts of God in every held land (src/sim/acts.ts): a failed harvest that eats a season and a half of the town's own eating from its stored food (ACTS.hunger), plague that stays two seasons, kills a tenth of the town's grown people each season from the record (the old first, never a child), thins an army kept there and passes next door, and fire that burns a share of the town's food and wood, is rebuilt out of the town's own stores and leaves the land's town damaged for the next battle; nothing is charged to the nation besides; all by the struck town's own standing (townfaith.ts), a hard strike rare; none of famine or fire under rain, twice as many under dust; marked on the map and told in the log
  • Crowding breeds plague (ACTS.crowd): a town of twenty people or more is struck, passed to and killed as before; a smaller one in proportion to its people, so a hamlet of four is struck a fifth as often, passed to a fifth as often, and loses a fraction of one person a season where before it always lost one. Plague's dead are noted in the season's ledger and on the season's card
  • A harvest fails by chance: about once in ten minutes half of an ordinary town's fields grow nothing for two minutes, by creed like the rest (a third as often, one field in six and forty seconds for the Righteous); twice as often in dust, never in heavy rain; the town is told
  • Famine: a food store bare for a minute leaves a town hungry, and after two it starves, losing one of its people every half minute (the old, then soldiers who walk away, then grown villagers, never the last four; never a child); after four minutes plague breaks out round the Town Center, three times as late among the Righteous; food in the store ends it at once; a war band without a hall and the armies of a field battle do not starve
  • Plague has a third source: famine
  • Fire spreads as the rule says: a seven-in-a-hundred chance each second for each building within reach (it had been all but settled once for each pair of buildings, so most never caught). Measured in a town of thirty-six houses a tile apart, one lit in a dust storm: left to itself 34 catch, none burns down and an eighth of the town's strength is lost in 41 seconds; with twelve villagers standing by 25 catch and under a fiftieth is lost
  • Fighting a fire: villagers right-clicked onto a burning building of their own beat it down before mending it, up to five to a fire, each taking a second and a half off it every second; idle villagers within nine tiles turn out unasked, and the panel counts who is at it

  • A failed harvest strikes only fields: a town living by hunting, fishing or its herds is never caught short by chance

  • The computer player does nothing about hunger beyond what it already does for food: in three matches of twenty-five minutes its store was never bare for more than 31 seconds, but it has no rule for a famine if one came

A man at war is still of his house

Working, rough

A man who leaves to train or march stays a member of his household (the owner's decision; src/sim/household.ts).

  • When training takes a husband, his house keeps his place: he is still its man, nobody else is moved in, and he is struck from it only when he dies
  • An unmarried son with no house of his own stays on the record of the house he was born in
  • Every foe a man of the house fells in war is counted to the house, and every three raise its renown a rank, beside the children it has raised (so more room and children sooner, as renown always gave)
  • His absence is felt: he works no field, and the house begins no child while its man or its woman is away
  • The house panel names who is away, as what, what he has felled, and what the house has won in war
  • Between seasons the town's record keeps the house: a notable woman's man by name and what her house has won; a home's standing counts three foes felled as one child raised, so a house can be known for its men's deeds; when he falls he is struck from the record and what he won stays
  • Coming home: the next time the town turns out for a battle or a visit, a notable woman and her man are under a roof together by their own names, with the children raised and the war record to the house's name
  • A woman of the house who goes to serve as a healer is kept the same way
  • On the map (`src/sim/warhouse.ts`): every man a town has with the armies has a name and a house of that town on the town's record. A company raised, by the player, by a town left training or by a rival, takes an unmarried man before a husband (the men a town has beyond its women are its sons); a house gives two sons at the most before another is asked; the man of a house the nation knows goes by his own name
  • A man who falls in a battle on the map is his house's loss: the house keeps his name among its fallen, the log names him, and the town grieves a season for each man lost and one more (four at the most). Where his house is one the nation knows he is listed with the town's lost notable people, so the loss weighs on the town and the nation as theirs does and the roll and the season's report name him; his wife's record no longer has a man

Threats of men on the map

Working, rough

Bands that belong to no nation choose a town and strike it; the town defends itself, and the player is given a report.

  • One band is meant to be about for every six held lands, never fewer than one, and one more while any people is Worldly or Decadent; a missing one appears in a season as often as not, from the third season
  • A band strikes the softest town in its reach a little over half the seasons, and a decadent people draws it more
  • The town defends itself with what it has: its grown men, its fortress and barracks, the towers and walls on its battlefield by how whole they are, an army kept there, a capital's guard, all stiffened or slackened by its people's creed, and weakened by plague
  • Three outcomes: held off (the band is weakened, a weak one broken), raided (men die at the walls, the town's stores looted and nothing more charged to the nation, a farm may burn), taken (all that and people carried off: women by raiders and pirates, men and women by slavers). Stories inside the story

Working, rough

Personal matters that come by chance out of the running systems, play out over seasons through named people, and leave a mark on the record (src/sim/intrigue.ts, src/sim/courtstory.ts).

  • A thief: in a town of the player's with gold in store, somebody keeps robbing the same three houses, one a season, and gold goes from the town's store; likelier where the town is hungry or has people at the tavern
  • The leader may set a watch on those houses, paid by the town each season and surer every season it is kept; a thief who is taken stands for judgement with the reason they stole (hunger, a soldier's widow, drink, greed) and is hanged, banished, set to work it off, or pardoned, each with its own cost to the people's piety and its own share of the gold got back; left three seasons without a judgement, the town's elders set them to work it off
  • A killer: in a town of eight grown people or more a grown person is found dead, then others; five grown people are suspected by name, and each season a clue may clear one of them (or, once, everyone of one sex), surer with the leader's court or an army in the town
  • The leader may accuse any suspect at any time: rightly, and the killer hangs and it is over; wrongly, and an innocent hangs, the town knows it, and the killings go on. Left with one suspect the town acts itself; left long enough the killer is never found. A town is never emptied by it
  • Each story is on its town's panel with the people by name and face, the clues found and the choices as buttons; what each did is in the season's report; however it ends, a line goes in the chronicle
  • A rival king (or queen): while the leader has a living spouse and a rival people is neither ally nor at truce, its ruler may send word that they will have the leader's head and the spouse for their own; two seasons on comes the demand, to be answered in two: defy it, buy them off for 6 supplies (it holds half the time), or send the spouse over the border for eight seasons' truce at a great cost in piety and in the loyalty of the inner circle. Nothing is told of the spouse after
  • Defied (or left unanswered), the rival sends a knife one to three seasons on: it is taken at the door 35% of the time, 35% likelier with a guard kept about the leader (1 supply a season) and 15% likelier with a loyal marshal or right hand; otherwise the leader is wounded, or one time in five killed, and the succession follows as for any fallen leader
  • A suitor: a son of a rival's ruling house asks for a daughter of the leader who is of age (never one under age). The leader may ask her what she wants, then give her (ten seasons' truce and goodwill; she leaves the house and can no longer follow the leader; against her will it costs piety and loyalty), decline with gifts, or insult him, which costs all goodwill; left three seasons without an answer he takes it as an insult
  • A plot: while a noble of the player's is angry (nobles.ts) one may begin to plot for the leader's place, hidden among four who are spoken of. Each season something may come out that clears one of them: likelier with a loyal confidant (kin.ts plotWarning; a disloyal one hides it), with informers kept for 1 supply a season, or with the court in the plotter's town. The leader may accuse one (wrongly, and that noble's mood falls by 40), or send gifts to every noble house for 4 supplies, which ends it if the plotter is sweetened enough
  • A plotter found is pardoned (piety up, the other nobles pleased) or put to death (piety down, the others soured; a captain or matron is struck from the town's record, one of the inner circle leaves it). Left six seasons unfound it ends in a rising: put down, or, about a third of the time and likelier for each other angry noble, the leader is killed in it
  • The confidant's warning also moves the odds of stopping the rival's knife, up or down by as much as 15%
  • One story of the house at a time, none before the twelfth season, sixteen seasons between them; one story of a town at a time for the whole nation
  • No child is ever robbed, suspected, accused, courted, killed or named, and tests hold the stories to it
  • What is decided carries on for years (src/sim/aftermath.ts), each as a mark kept with the town, noble or people it is about and shown on the land's panel: a pardoned thief's town warms (its nobles' moods up 4 a season and its faith up, for 12 seasons) and one hanged for stealing to eat sours as long; a killer found eases the town for 6 seasons; an innocent hanged for a killing leaves it resentful for 20
  • A rival king defied is a feud for 16 seasons: his people bear no goodwill and come for the player before anyone else. A king bought off comes back for the spouse 8 to 12 seasons on
  • A daughter wed is an alliance that can be called on once, from the court: her husband's people send two companies to the leader's army, or refuse, likelier to send the more goodwill there is
  • A pardoned plotter, 5 to 9 seasons on, turns out the staunchest of the nobles for 20 seasons, or plots again; nobody is shown which until it happens

Armies belong to towns

Working, rough

An army is the men of the town that raised it (src/sim/armytown.ts).

  • Raising a company takes up to two men of working age, the youngest first, off the town's record and onto the army's roll, each at his own age; a town keeps two men at the least and a man for every other woman of working age, so it still has husbands
  • A company its town cannot man in full is filled from the other towns of its people, the town with most men to spare first, each within what a town keeps (two men, and a man for every other woman); only when no town can spare a man are a company's men of no town. Shared report, 96 campaigns: men under arms 3.3% in war and 0% in peace before, 19.2% and 5.6% after (target 10 to 25%, under 10%); towns that die out 12.9 and 13.9 per 100, and 14.5 in a trial that kept more husbands home and sent fewer men, so that figure does not follow the men sent
  • When an army loses companies, men are lost to it in that share, drawn by lot across the towns they came from, and an army broken loses them all; but not all are dead: three in ten die when the army still stands and four in ten when it is broken, and the rest come home to their towns hurt or routed (those whose town is no longer their people's have no home to come to). The log says how many of which town fell and how many came home
  • Armies that join share out their men as they share out their companies
  • An army that has stormed a town rests there six seasons before it marches again, the player's as a rival's: this is what sets the pace of a campaign
  • An army standing in a town of its own people, without the leader, can be sent home: it is disbanded, its men go back onto their towns' records a season older for every season away, and its named soldier is one of the notable people of the town most of them came from
  • A soldier who makes a name in a battle fought in person is remembered in the town his army came from, not always the chief town
  • The campaign screen says how many men of a town are away with the armies

Gaps

  • A company is still raised when its town has not two men for it: the shortfall are men of no town, so men still come from nowhere. Allowed for now by the owner's word; refusing the order would stop most campaigns as towns are sized today
  • Armies that begin the campaign, volunteers, and every army in a campaign saved before this have no roll and are men of no town
  • Rivals raise, lose and never send home; they take no account of their towns' men
  • A man with an army is not tied to his house (household.ts works inside a battle or visit only), and men with the armies are not counted in a town's output
  • Men of no town who are sent home vanish; they are not settled anywhere
  • The men who fall are counted, not named
  • In 8 computer-played campaigns (3 rivals, up to 60 turns): 13 to 17 men fell per campaign, and at the end 0 to 4 men were away with armies, because so few towns have men to give. Untuned

  • A computer-led nation sends an army that can reach an open town of another people to take it, and those who followed the army settle it (two men and two women), so it lives again; nobody is there to resent it

A town taken

Working, rough

A town taken by force resents it, and settles or rises (src/sim/conquered.ts).

  • Resentment is a state on the town, 0 to 100: 80 when taken from a nation, 65 from a tribe, 20 more for a chief town; a town taken back by its own people is free of it
  • It eases 1 a season, and faster under good rule: a Church (+3), Farmland (+2), the leader in the town (+4), a pious people (+2), and 3 more in any season an army of its holder stands in it; a Tavern (2) and a season in which the town was struck by plague, famine, fire or raiders (6) set it back
  • Above 15 the town may rise each season, by the square of its resentment (steeply: likely at 40, certain from 45). An army in the town all but rules it out, and for eight seasons after an army leaves the town is cowed and does not rise at all, so risings come late and in towns long left bare; the Righteous halve the chance, the Decadent add half, and with the leader there it does not rise
  • A rising with no army there throws the taker off: the town goes back to the people it was taken from if they stand, otherwise to its own tribe; it is in the log and the chronicle
  • A rising with an army there is put down: a man of the town dies, the garrison loses a company, and resentment goes up 10
  • At nothing the town has settled and the log says so; it is remembered as taken for five years, and for that long the common run of unrest does not touch it: it rises by its resentment or not at all
  • A resentful town's people wear the angry face, and the campaign screen shows the resentment and the chance of a rising

Gaps

  • Tuned against docs/TUNING.md with the shared report as fixed on main (it follows every capture once): taken towns rise 21.3% of the time when left without an army (target 15 to 35%) and 0.0% with one in them over half the time (target under 5%); on 48 campaigns 19.5% and 1.1%. The garrisoned figure had read 11%: a rising put down was marked as common unrest, and the older rule then threw the town off the next season if the army had gone
  • Resentment costs the holder nothing but the risk: a resentful town yields and fights as any other
  • Its people are not kept as another people within the town, and there is no way to win them faster by what is done in a visit
  • The older unrest (by chance, in any land) still strikes towns that were never taken, or were taken more than five years ago
  • Rivals do not garrison or build with resentment in mind
  • A nation whose last town is given back to it by a rising, after it was counted as beaten, is not brought back to life

Faith is a town's

Working, rough

Every town has its own standing between piety and decadence; the nation's is its towns' taken together (src/sim/townfaith.ts).

  • Each town has its own standing on the same scale; a town nobody has asked after stands where its nation does, so older campaigns load unchanged
  • The nation's standing is the mean of its towns', kept up to date, so everything that read a nation's creed still works and now reads its towns
  • A Church, a Tavern and a holy place move the town they stand in, each season, and no other
  • A town defended or visited in person begins at its own standing and it is that town the result moves; an attacking army still takes what it became home to all
  • A raid sways the town struck; a tale answered or a comet moves every town
  • A town taken keeps its own standing, so taking a Decadent town makes a pious nation less so
  • Anything that still sets a nation's figure by hand moves every town by the difference
  • The campaign screen shows each town's own creed
  • The pull: every season each town is drawn 10% of the way toward where its nation stands, so a Church or a Tavern (3 a season) holds its town 25 to 30 from the nation and no further; towns with neither come to stand with their nation

Gaps

  • The mean counts every town alike, a hamlet as much as a capital

  • The strength of the pull (15% a season) is a first choice. In 8 computer-played campaigns with it, untuned, the widest gap between two towns of one nation was 6, 31, 33, 34, 55, 56 and 78 in campaigns of 5 to 18 turns and 103 in one of 56 turns (before the pull: 12 to 36 in short campaigns, 108 to 200 in long ones). The widest gaps are in nations that have just taken towns of another creed, which the pull has not yet drawn in
  • Acts of God, births and a town's defence against raiders still read the nation's creed, not the town's
  • With several towns, one Church moves the nation a fraction of what it did: the old numbers hold only for a nation of one town
  • `virtueDrift` still reports the nation-wide pressure (all churches less all taverns), which is no longer what the nation moves by

Studies of rule, law, nobles and faith

First pass

  • Stewardship (stewardship.ts): a town's governor finishes what he has begun half again as fast

Nobles

First pass

Some people rise into a noble class by what they did or the post they hold, have a say in decrees, and their moods tell on their towns (src/sim/nobles.ts; docs/DESIGN.md, "Relationships").

  • Read each season from what is kept: the inner circle (kin.ts), each town's most famous veteran as its captain and most famous mother as its matron, and a lord for every town of twelve grown or more, named from the seed and new when the town changes hands
  • Each has a leaning (pious, martial, worldly, kindly; the priest pious, the marshal martial, the steward worldly) and a mood, -100 to 100, that eases a fifth of the way back each season and sours in a town raided, hungry or sick
  • Every decree pleases some and angers others by leaning, fully in their own town and a third as much elsewhere; the land's panel names the town's nobles and how they feel, and each decree button says whom it would please and anger
  • An angry noble's town brings in 5% less of its harvest, drifts the readier, and keeps a fifth less of its watch against bands while the leader is not there
  • The circle are nobles: a decree moves their loyalty, and the mood of their town's nobles draws on it each season
  • Rivals have nobles by the same rules, and their sensible decrees move them
  • A town whose lord dies, is put to death or flees has a new one at once, of another name and with no grudge (`newLord`; a plot's end uses it)
  • Tested in `tests/nobles.test.ts`

Gaps

  • The computer's rulers do not weigh their nobles when they decree
  • A noble cannot plot, ask, be raised or be put down by the player; only decrees move them
  • Nobles have no faces of their own beyond the notable people's
  • The leanings and how much each decree moves them are guesses, not tuned

Stores replace supplies

Working, rough

What a nation has to spend on the map is what its towns hold in store (src/sim/stores.ts).

  • Every town keeps a working stock of 600 goods that the map does not touch; everything its towns hold above that, taken together, is the nation's to spend, and one supply is a hundred of goods (40 food, 30 wood, 15 gold, 15 stone)
  • Every price, income and loss on the map is still counted in supplies, and is now carried into the towns: what is spent comes out of their stores (most from the towns with most to give), what is earned is shared among them
  • A new nation's supplies are goods in its chief town from the first, so a campaign begins with the numbers it always had; a saved campaign is laid down the same way when it is loaded
  • What a town gathers in a battle or a visit makes the nation richer, and what it burns through makes it poorer: a town fights out of everything it holds, the nation's spare goods included
  • A town run below its working stock (sacked, or spent bare in a siege) is made good out of income before there is anything to spend; the campaign screen says how far short the towns are
  • A town lost takes what was in it, and whoever takes it has it (less the half that is looted when it falls)
  • A people with no town has no stores
  • The campaign screen shows the nation's stores by kind and what there is to spend
  • Prices by kind (src/sim/prices.ts): a thing is paid in what it is made of, at its old price in supplies: farmland, a Barracks, a Range or a Harbour in wood; a Stable in wood and gold; a Church in stone and gold; a Tavern in wood and gold; a Fortress or a road over a pass in stone; a bridge in wood and stone; a company in gold, food and some wood; a leader hired, a truce, an alliance, a gift, a tribute or a tale's price in gold; a new age in all four. It is taken from the town where it is done first, then from the towns with most of that kind; what no town has of the kind is made up in other goods one for one (SHORT_RATE = 1, the owner's decision: held strictly to its kinds, a new nation could not raise its first Range, 500 wood against 440), so a price is never more than it was. The build and raise buttons say what a thing is paid in
  • Income by land (src/sim/harvest.ts), the only place a town earns: each land's yield goes into its own town when the season turns. How much is the land's measure (its farms, country and weather) as far as the town has the hands to work it (full with 6 grown men and women, nothing with none). What it is made of is where the people work (the governor's shares, by the last orders or his own, and the fields when food is short), weighed by what a hand brings in there and by what the land leans to (food on fertile or coastal land, gold from gold hills, stone from hills, wood from forest). What a people earns as a whole, and the yield of a land the marauders still sit in, go into its chief town; a store is full at 20,000 and the rest is lost
  • Convoys by road (src/sim/convoys.ts): a town sends food, goods or people (a man and a woman, the youngest it can spare, never its last man or woman and never a child) to another of its own towns by a road through its own lands, or across a sea lane where either end has a Harbour (and there pirates stop it as highwaymen do on a road), a season on the way, kept in a saved game; highwaymen on its road stop it three times in four and take half of every store, never the people, unless an army of its people on the road is as strong as they are; a convoy whose town is lost on the way goes to the chief town. The player sends one from a town's panel (food, goods or a couple); the computer sends 300 food to any town of its own with less than 150
  • Losses are counted once: an act of God or a raid takes from the town struck and nothing from the nation besides; a capital sacked loses only what is in it, to whoever takes it
  • In 24 computer-run campaigns of 60 seasons (three rivals, the player let be), against main before convoys, prices by kind and income by land: wars about as long (mean 34.7 seasons against 35.5; 5 undecided against 3); rivals' supplies higher late on (877 against 649 at the sixtieth season, with losses no longer counted twice) and their companies more (45.6 against 37.9); the biggest town larger (32,500 in store against 25,600), income now gathering in the lands that yield it; a rival town short of food hardly ever (1 in about 390 at the tenth and twentieth seasons, none after); 89 convoys sent by the computer

Gaps

  • Spending still draws on any town at once, wherever it is spent: only what is sent on purpose goes by road
  • Gains other than the season's yield (a hoard, a fine harvest, a prize, a gift received, a tale's reward) are still shared evenly among a nation's towns
  • Convoys are not drawn on the map, and the computer sends only food, never goods or people
  • Plague on the map now takes people (209 died of it across the 24 trial campaigns), and 60 of 227 famines emptied the town's granary
  • The working stock (600) and the worth of a supply (100) are guesses; in 5 computer-played campaigns of 8 to 41 turns no rival was left unable to spend, and none ended short of its working stock, but nothing was tuned
  • Rivals spend as they always did and take no account of which town holds what
  • Seen in a browser on the flat map: the stores and to-spend lines. No battle was played by hand to watch the figure move

Towns eat their food

Working, rough

Each season a town's people eat from its food store on the map; a town that cannot goes hungry, and one hungry long enough loses people (src/sim/larder.ts).

  • A grown man or woman eats 16 food a season, one of the old 12, a child 6; men away with an army are off the town's record already (armytown.ts), and those drifted to the mines or the tavern (drift.ts: a share of the men, and of the women where there is a tavern, as much as the town was strained) eat where they work
  • Food does not keep for ever: what a town holds beyond three seasons of its own eating, and beyond the 240 food of its working stock, loses a fifth of the excess each season (KEEP_SEASONS, FOOD_KEEP, ROT); a governor with a full granary and no orders sends half his field hands to work that keeps
  • A town that cannot eat in full eats what there is and goes hungry: its granary is empty, which drift.ts reads as strain; the player is told the first season
  • Hungry more than 2 seasons running, a town loses a tenth of its grown people a season (one at least), the old first, never a child; each death is told in the log, and a town whose grown are all gone is written in the chronicle
  • A farmed town of twenty is fed by its own fields (292 food a season against the 204 it eats, its people working as a governor sets them); the same land without a farm yields 64, and lives on convoys or on food sent by decree
  • Eaten after convoys come in and before births, so a town fed by road can bear children that season; the local tribes' towns are not counted
  • Children taken in (src/sim/orphans.ts): a town left with no grown people, however it came about, sends its children each season, with their ages, to the nearest town of its people (as the crow flies: they are carried, over sea and closed borders too), as many as each can hold; told to the player and kept in the chronicle (docs/DESIGN.md, "Children are never shown")
  • A people with no other town: its last children are taken in by the nearest other people (a living nation or the local tribes, never the marauders), join that town's children on its record and grow up as its people; the player is told whether the children were theirs or are theirs now
  • In 24 computer-run campaigns of 60 seasons (three rivals, the player let be), with one earning and one eating: 199 of 14,480 town-seasons hungry, 197 of them a rival's; farmed towns hungry in 0.8% of their seasons, towns without a farm in 6%; 89 towns hungry at some time; 14 people died of hunger, none the idle player's; 3 towns starved out; 275 convoys sent by the computer; wars 37.6 seasons, 4 undecided. (Before, with the governor earning and eating on top: 166 hungry, 28 dead, 1 starved out, 184 convoys, 38.4 seasons; before the governor: 187 hungry, 35 dead, 1 starved out)

Gaps

  • A town grows by births until its food binds, then goes hungry and loses people: an idle player's capital did so in several trials, with nothing to warn of it before the granary is empty
  • Eating is by head only: no season, no weather and no creed changes how much a town eats
  • Battles and visits still feed their people by the battle's own rules (famine.ts), not by these shares

Food and health buildings

Working, rough

Buildings and studies that answer hunger and sickness, on the map and in a town (docs/DESIGN.md, "Buildings and research that answer the systems"): a Granary, a Mill, a Well and an Infirmary (src/sim/granary.ts, mill.ts, well.ts, infirmary.ts), and Crop Rotation, Salting and Smoking, Quarantine, Midwifery and Physicians (croprotation.ts, salting.ts, quarantine.ts, midwifery.ts, physicians.ts).

  • Granary: on the map, food in store spoils a season of eating later and a failed harvest draws three fifths of what it would on the store; in a town, a bare store takes twice as long to turn to hunger and to famine
  • Mill: on the map, a land with Farmland yields two fifths more food; in a town, farms are worked two fifths faster
  • Well: on the map, a crowded town counts three fifths as crowded for plague (it breaks out, passes to it and kills less); in a town, plague passes among a people's own half as readily
  • Infirmary (Bronze Age): on the map, plague takes three fifths of those it would and a grieving town grieves two seasons less each season; in a town, plague wears the sick down three fifths as fast and a grieving house mourns twice as fast

  • The studies (Bronze Age, Physicians Iron Age and after Herbalism; studies.ts: learned in a town where the building that teaches them stands, kept by the nation, known in a visit): Crop Rotation at the Mill, harvests fail three fifths as often on the map and fields three fifths as often in a town; Salting and Smoking at the Granary, laid-by food spoils half as fast on the map and a bare store lasts half again as long in a town; Quarantine at the Infirmary, plague crosses between towns half as often where either keeps it, and is caught from strangers half as readily in a town; Midwifery at the Infirmary, three quarters of the births that would not come still do not; Physicians at the Infirmary, plague takes four fifths on the map and healers heal half again as fast in a town. Paid in wood and gold, never bread
  • The governor begins the buildings, in the player's towns and the rivals' alike, out of plenty (src/sim/plenty.ts, the one rule for every work and study a governor or a rival buys: the town has its Farmland, is not short of bread, wants no roof or wall, and keeps four times the governor's floor in goods other than bread after paying): a Mill when the town has sixteen people, a Granary after hunger or a failed harvest, a Well in a crowded town where plague has lately been near, an Infirmary in a town of fourteen or more after plague or in grief. Rivals learn the studies by what presses on their towns (studies.ts; births that did not come press for Midwifery). The player's governors learn none for them
  • At 96 campaigns against main edcf706 run the same way: a farmed town is hungry 1.5% of its seasons (main 2.2%, out of band), deaths from hunger 0.8 a nation in twenty years (1.0), towns dying out 1.28 per 100 (1.11); the rest as it was. Sensible decrees against none is out of band on both (27.0 against 27.8 a town with decrees): these buildings ease what the decrees answer, in towns given none as well. Built or learned without care they did harm: a Granary that kept two seasons more, studies paid in bread, or works taking the land's places, each sent a farmed town's hunger to 2.2 to 2.8% (against an earlier main's 1.4%)

A town left to itself

Working, rough

A town carries on with its last orders, and a governor fills the gaps (src/sim/governor.ts).

Battles fought on the map

Working, rough

A battle between nations that nobody watches is fought out by what the two sides are, a round at a time, and no longer settled by setting one sum against another (src/sim/mapbattle.ts).

  • The map's own weights for the attack and the defence are fought out in rounds: each kind of company strikes with its own strength and harder against the kind it beats, and what lands falls hardest on the kind being beaten
  • A town's own defence (walls, towers, the Fortress, its ground, its men on the walls) is its works: it strikes, takes its share of what lands, and so shelters the companies behind it
  • A side breaks when it has lost half of what it began with; veterans, a leader with the army, walls and a steadfast creed hold longer, a slack creed gives sooner. If neither breaks in eight rounds the attack draws off
  • Losses are by kind. A beaten attacker keeps what is left where it marched from; a beaten garrison falls back on a land of its own next door and joins the army there; left with under a fifth of its companies, a broken side scatters for home
  • The men of the companies lost are their towns' and their houses' as before (armytown.ts, warhouse.ts), and what the victor felled is the companies it broke, not the whole army
  • Each battle is told in the log and kept on the campaign (the last twelve): who led, who broke and in which round, what each side lost by kind, and who fell by name
  • The computer's peoples choose where to march by the same fight on an even day (wouldTake), with their leader's caution added to the defence

Gaps

  • The computer cannot see what manned walls are worth beyond their sum, or the defender's home ground: it judges by the same fight without them. That is what keeps walled towns holding most sieges; told exactly, it attacks only sure things and they hold a quarter
  • Nations are seldom left with no army at all now, so men under arms in peace read just over the band (11.1% against under 10% at 192 campaigns): the stand-down rule (rivalrule.ts) was tuned when a beaten army was gone to the last man
  • The tales are in the log and on the campaign, with no panel of their own on the map yet
  • A tribe's land and the marauders' are fought the same way with the tribe's warriors as infantry; a band fight (bandfight.ts) still goes by its own means
  • A battle the player fights in person is untouched: its losses are who lived on the field
  • Reserves next door add weight, not companies of a kind
  • Every figure (the bite of a round, where a side breaks, the rout) is a first setting

Unrest

Working, rough

Unrest begins in a town by that town's own state, not by one draw for the whole world (src/sim/unrest.ts).

  • Each season every town that could stir is asked by its own chance: 8 in 100 before its state is weighed, and never more than 25
  • What weighs on it: hungry, two and a half times as likely; a church, six tenths; Farmland, a little less; a tavern, a third more; under a pious people, a fifth less; newly held, 1.6 times, easing over forty seasons to 0.6 for a town long kept; an angry noble, twice; and its nobles' mood otherwise, three tenths either way
  • No unrest in a capital, a town with its people's army or leader in it, an empty town, one already troubled, or a town taken by force until it has settled and five years have passed (until then its troubles are its resentment's account, conquered.ts; let in sooner, taken towns with an army in them rose 23.5% of the time for 1.3%); a steadfast people's towns keep faith
  • Once a town is in unrest it is as it was: two seasons, and an army there, the leader or the leader's right hand keeps order, or it rises
  • A town's panel says its chance and what makes it so
  • The overall rate was kept: in 96 computer-played campaigns the old draw began 1,712 bouts of unrest and 1,596 towns rose; this begins 1,496 and 1,455 rise

Gaps

  • Making it the town's own matter did not make fewer worlds fall apart: on 192 campaigns on main 30ed26b, ruin and undecided together are 13 before and 13 after (2 in ruin and 11 undecided, then 5 and 8); a campaign is decided at 68 and 71 seasons, taken towns rise 19.9% and 19.8% ungarrisoned, 1.3% and 1.2% garrisoned
  • The weights are chosen figures, set so that the whole came out where the old draw had it; none was tuned alone
  • How long a town has been held is counted from when this rule first looked: in a campaign saved before it every town is taken as long held
  • A computer-led people still sends no army to a town in unrest: tried, and it left more worlds undecided, because the armies it pins stop taking land (see A world that ends in ruin)
  • The panel line was not seen in a browser

A world that ends in ruin

Working, rough

A world where no people has gained ground for twenty years while the bands hold much of the map ends with no winner (the owner's word; src/sim/ruin.ts).

  • Once a season, where campaigns are decided: if no living people holds more land than it held 80 seasons before, the bands held a fifth of the map or more (lands the marauders hold, and lands where a band has its place) in at least a quarter of those 80 seasons, and the peoples together hold less than half of it, the campaign is over with no winner
  • The bands' hold is read over the twenty years and not at the moment: read at the moment it came and went with every band broken or newly camped, the count of ruin endings in 96 campaigns swung between 0 and 4 on changes that had nothing to do with it, and worlds where the peoples held a sixth of the map stayed open because the bands stood at 17% that season
  • The chronicle closes the age in a line, its last page says the land lay in ruin, the ending screen is headed The land lies in ruin, and the menu lists the world as over and not won
  • A land taken and lost again is not ground gained; a people that has died out is not waited for; a campaign saved before this begins its record of ground the first season
  • The player is warned when ruin is under twenty seasons off: a line in the season's report and on the nation bar, with the seasons left, withdrawn if some people gains ground and keeps it
  • Shared report, 96 campaigns on main ee829bc: 1 ended in ruin (seed 6, season 179, the peoples holding 9 of 23 lands); undecided at 200 seasons 3.1%, a campaign decided at 74 seasons. Of 32 worlds with a passive player, 1 ends in ruin (seed 5, season 206) where a rival would have conquered it later; all 32 end with or without the rule

Gaps

  • How the owner's words are read is this module's choice: ground gained and kept over twenty years, not any land taken (towns change hands with tribes and bands most seasons, and no world went 20 seasons without one); and the peoples holding under half the map. Without the second, 7 of 96 campaigns ended in ruin and five of those were strong nations at a stand-off
  • The worlds that stalled for a thousand seasons when this was asked for no longer stall on today's main (a poor people now musters, armies redeploy, plague needs crowding), so the rule is shown on a built world in tests and on one campaign in each of the two samples, not on those seeds
  • docs/TUNING.md asks that ruin be rare, under 3 campaigns in 100. On 96 campaigns on main 314d839 it is 5 (5.2%, at seasons 137 to 192) with 1 left undecided; read at the moment it was 1 with 6 undecided. So about six worlds in a hundred are ruined or stuck by 200 seasons whichever way it is read, and the rule decides only whether they are called so. With the peoples' share asked to be under two-fifths it is 4 and 2. Bringing it under 3 means fewer worlds falling apart, which is not this rule's to do
  • What takes land from the peoples in a ruined world's last twenty years (2 ruined worlds of 96, 58 lands lost): 64% towns rising and throwing their ruler off, 29% war among the peoples, 7% marauders; across all 96 campaigns it is 32%, 64% and 3%. Four risings in five are not the resentment of a taken town but the older lottery of unrest (campaign.ts: three seasons in ten some ungarrisoned town is in unrest, and rises in two unless an army or the leader is there), which is the same number of risings for a world of weak peoples as for a strong one; in ruined worlds the rulers had a median of 5 companies against 22. Tried and withdrawn: a computer-led people sending its nearest army to a town in unrest. On 96 campaigns on main 230aba0 it read 3 in ruin and 5 undecided against none of either without it, because the armies it pins stop taking land
  • Neither the warning nor the ending screen was seen in a browser

Going to marry

Working, rough

A town left with grown people of one sex only, or very few of one, draws the unmarried of its people's other towns, by road (src/sim/suitors.ts).

  • A town with no grown men (those away with its armies counted) or no grown women draws up to two of that sex; a town where one sex outnumbers the other three to one draws one
  • The unmarried go first: the men a town has beyond its women, or the women beyond its men, and never its last at home; a town that wants for one sex itself sends nobody
  • To a town with no men at all, where its people have no unmarried man anywhere, a town sends one of the men it could have spared to an army (it keeps two men, and a man for every other woman). Shared report, 96 campaigns on main fa70b9e, without and with this: towns that die out 5.7 and 4.9 per 100 (128 and 108 towns), towns all one sex 4.2% and 3.6%; of the towns that died out 93% and 92% had been all one sex in their last two years, 32% and 31% had changed hands, and plague (58%, 61%) and hunger (39%, 34%) took the last of them
  • They go as a convoy goes (convoys.ts): through the people's own lands, a season on the way, and are of the town they come to from then on; nobody sets out for a town while others are on the road to it; two parties a people a season at the most
  • Every nation's towns do it; the player is told who set out, from where, to where and why, and a town's panel says what it wants for
  • With it, the two places war took a town's last man by rule no longer do: the toll of a battle nobody watched (township.ts) and a rising put down (conquered.ts) leave a town with women in it one man
  • Shared report, 96 campaigns on main e2219b1: towns all one sex in a nation 12.8% before, 12.0% with going to marry alone, 6.4% with the toll's last man, 4.2% with the put-down's (target under 5%); towns that die out 7.8, 8.8, 6.6 and 5.9 per 100

Gaps

  • Going to marry does little by itself: in 24 campaigns, of the town-seasons with no men 84% were of a nation with no unmarried man in any town, and 12% had one and no road. Men are short everywhere, three women or more to a man in a fifth of town-seasons; what moved the figure was sparing the last man
  • What still empties a town of men: its last man growing old, plague, and hunger; none of those is touched here
  • Nobody is asked: the unmarried of a town go whether the player would have kept them or not, and the player cannot forbid it
  • They are not married to anyone by name on arrival; the town's couples are a count (seasonfolk.ts)
  • A town left alone read 1.20x after 20 years in the last run (1.3 to 2 wanted, 1.33 and 1.25 in the two runs before): within what runs differ by, and not shown to follow from this
  • Not seen in a browser

How a rival rules its towns

Working, rough

What a computer-led nation does so that its towns live by the same rules, and so that it rules them with an eye to how they are (src/sim/rivalrule.ts).

  • The player's unvisited towns are laid down on record the same way as a rival's, so neither side is spared the room of its houses and both governors build; the first battle fought there in person, or the first visit, puts the real record in its place
  • A rival's towns have buildings on record, laid down coarsely: a hall, the works the map knows, and roofs for the people there and one to spare; so they are held to the room of their houses and their governors build more. The record is for counting only, never where things stand in a battle, and is dropped when the town leaves the rival's hands
  • An army that has stood four seasons in the same land of its own, with no foreign land beside it, is sent home and its men go back to their towns; a rival always keeps one army
  • Soldiers make a name on the map: any army that has won twice has one, remembered in the town its men came from, lost if the army is destroyed and kept if it is sent home. This holds for the player's armies settled from the map as well
  • A notable home arises in a rival's town with three children or more on its record
  • A rival gives each town its orders every season: hands to whatever the town is shortest of
  • It garrisons a town that is resentful enough to rise, or has a band upon it, from an army beside it; an army holding such a town behind the lines stays there, while one at the front goes on with the war
  • It does not raise a company out of a strained town that is down to its last two men
  • War and peace: a nation is at war for four seasons after it wins or loses land; at peace it keeps a standing force of 5% of its men (8% until the figure in peace read 6.3% to 9.1% from run to run against a limit of 10%; at 5% it read 4.9%, with 15.8% in war, on 96 campaigns on main 381c6fa) and sends the rest home (whole armies, and companies of the leader's); after ten seasons of peace it musters for a campaign for eight, and stands down again if nothing comes of it. This holds for whoever the computer plays, a rival or the player's side in a trial; a human player's armies are left alone
  • A rival lives in an age twenty seasons before it takes the next, however full its stores (`mayAdvance`); its first age waits only on its stores, and the player is not held to it. Without this a rich rival took a second age within 2 to 13 seasons of its first. Shared report, 96 campaigns: a rival's next age 19 before, 21 after (target 20 to 60), a campaign decided at 71 and 75 seasons, with as many advances as before
  • A poor people still musters (`poorMuster`): a rival at war or mustering that cannot pay for a company out of what its towns can spare raises one a season all the same, out of the stores of one town and for no more than half of what that town holds, its bread last; at a town with a yard, or with none left at its chief town as plain infantry; its men within what a town keeps. Found in a world where a ruined rival with 25 men in its last town sat 1,400 seasons unable to raise a spear: that world now ends at season 134. Of 32 passive-player worlds 31 end and a rival wins 30 (29 before). Shared report, 96 campaigns on main 8ef884a: a campaign decided at 80 seasons (72 on the baseline), undecided 5.2% (6.3%), a rival's next age 22 (21), men under arms 20.0% in war and 6.9% in peace (18.8%, 5.6%), towns that die out 7.8 per 100 (12.8)

Gaps

  • The owner keeps the difficulty scaling of a rival's income as it is
  • With the player's towns laid down too, in 4 quiet campaigns of up to 80 seasons (untuned): the player's one town was at the limit of its room in 8 and 4 of 80 seasons in the two that grew, the governor built 7 and 3 houses, and the town ended at 60 people against 61 and 49 against 52 without the record; in the other two nothing changed (one town dwindled to 2 people either way, which is not this rule's doing)
  • An army with the leader in it is never moved to garrison a town
  • Armies too weak to take anything alone march to the strongest and join it, when their nation is at war or mustering; and a people whose towns have no men left calls its men home from its armies
  • The men step, measured with the shared report (24 campaigns): a town left alone after twenty years 0.45x before and 1.00x after (target 1.3 to 2); towns dying out 20.4 per 100 before and 19.4 after; nations' towns all one sex 30.0% before and 27.2% after; men under arms in war 18.2% before and 6.7% after (the floor on raising costs this; left there by the gathering session's word). So armies were why towns shrank, and are not why they die out or are left all one sex
  • Tuned with the shared report (24 campaigns): men under arms 9.1% in war and 25.0% in peace before, 16.7% and 0.0% after (targets 10 to 25%, under 10%). Pace: a campaign decided at a median of 62 seasons (was 28; target 60 to 160) and a rival's next age every 23 (was 14; target 20 to 60)
  • The raising guard is narrow on purpose: refusing every town's last men stops rivals raising armies at all as towns are sized today (tried: a campaign that ended in 36 turns did not end in 1,500)
  • In 8 computer-played campaigns (5 to 32 turns), untuned: every rival town but the newest was on record; governors built 2 houses in all (the coarse record starts with a roof to spare); 11 armies were sent home; rivals ended with 3 to 7 living named soldiers and up to 9 lost; 8 to 25 notable homes; 2 to 6 of their 8 to 12 resentful towns had an army in them
  • Notable homes on the map are named by rule, one to a town, not by which house did most
  • A rival's orders look only at the town's own stores, not at what its nation is short of
  • Feeding a hungry town and decrees are other modules' (convoys.ts, decrees.ts) and were already done for rivals

Rivals live by the same rules

Working, rough

An audit of what a rival nation's towns go through (tests/rivals.test.ts).

  • Checked by playing campaigns with the computer: every rival town has people on record at their own ages, births and coming of age each season, stores, its own faith, resentment when taken by force, raids by bands, a governor, and companies raised out of its men; a rival's supplies and creed are its towns' figures
  • Found and fixed in the audit: a battle's toll and a rising put down took men off a town's count without taking them off its record of ages
  • Where a rival marches (`src/sim/rivalaim.ts`): of the lands in reach it wants the one that is softest once the country and the faith are weighed. The defence it would meet is divided by how an army fares attacking into that country (marsh 0.85, desert 0.9, steppe 1.1), a supply of the country's yield is worth a point of defence, and a town at a gap of 100 from the nation's own faith costs a point and a half. Whether it dares the march is still its strength against the defence, with the country's march now counted there as it is in the battle
  • It marches on the most wanted land it dares, not only on the most wanted of all (which, once country and faith were weighed, was not always the softest, so an army could stand still beside a land it could have taken)
  • An army on a border with nothing in reach it dares goes by its own people's lands, a land a season and no further than six, to a border where there is something it dares (`redeploy`). Before, it stood where it was for good. Of 96 computer-played campaigns 10 were undecided at 200 seasons; in six of them one people held nearly the land that wins and had armies of 9 to 12 companies standing beside rivals' capitals while lands defended at 1 to 3 lay two marches off. Shared report, 96 campaigns on main db69a5e: undecided 8.3% before and 5.2% after, a campaign decided at 73 and 79 seasons, a rival's next age 21 and 22, men under arms 17.1% and 17.9% in war, 6.8% and 8.3% in peace

Gaps

  • One world in 32 still never ends with a passive player (seed 6): two ruined rivals raise companies as fast as plague, hunger and the bands take them, and the player's town touches nobody's land. The cost of raising is not what holds it
  • A rival's income is still multiplied by the difficulty (0.8, 1 or 1.25)
  • Tales are put only to the player; a rival's towns are swayed by a fixed step instead
  • The weights a rival gives country and faith are chosen figures (a point of defence for a supply of yield, a point and a half for a faith 100 apart), tuned only so far as the shared report's figures stayed in band: on 96 campaigns a campaign decided at 67 seasons before and 73 after, undecided 6.3% and 8.3%, a rival's next age 22 and 22, men under arms in war 19.2% and 17.6%
  • A rival weighs only the land next to its army when it chooses; it now walks to another border when it dares nothing, but it does not plan a road or mass two armies for one assault
  • Of the 10 undecided worlds three were of another kind and are not reached by this: both rivals ruined, the bands holding much of the map, nobody with the men to take anything
  • A shorter count for winning by holding land (8 seasons for 10) was tried and moved undecided campaigns by one in 96; left at 10

Several campaigns

Working, rough

More than one world kept, each with its own history, and any of them played again (docs/DESIGN.md, Relationships).

  • Up to six worlds are kept side by side in the browser (src/sim/slots.ts): the first is the key every campaign was always saved under, so no saved game is lost, and the others sit beside it
  • The Campaign button lists them (src/client/slotspanel.ts), the most lately played first, each by its people and its ruling house, with the turn, the day it was last played and whether it was won or lost; Play goes back to it
  • A new world is begun without losing the others, from the menu or from New campaign on the map; with none kept, the button begins one at once as before
  • Delete asks twice; deleting the world being played moves to the most lately played of the rest; a world an older version saved is listed so it can be deleted
  • A battle or a visit belongs to the world it was begun from

Gaps

  • With all six places taken, New campaign on the map replaces the world being played (as it always did); the menu asks for one to be deleted first
  • Worlds are kept in this browser only: nothing is copied elsewhere, and clearing the browser's storage loses them all
  • A skirmish save is still a single one, beside the worlds

The season's report

Working, rough

A card on the map when a turn ends: what changed in your nation that season, and for whom.

  • When the season turns, a small card over the map says what changed in the player's nation since the last: children born and come of age, the dead of old age, of hunger and with the armies, those carried off, got back or held too long, every raid on its towns with how it went (the raid report folded in), who drifted to the tavern or the mines and who came back to work, towns that rose, were lost or were won, and notable people made or lost by name and face (src/sim/seasonreport.ts, src/client/seasoncard.ts)
  • Only lines with something in them; a season with nothing in it shows no card
  • Closed by its button, its corner cross, Escape or a click beside it, and not shown again
  • The season's own systems note births, coming of age, deaths of age and of hunger and the fallen as they happen (src/sim/ledger.ts), so the counts are what happened and not a difference of totals
  • Men killed in raids are in the count of the dead (from the raids' own reports, once) as well as in the raid's line, and the men a town loses in a battle settled on the map are among the fallen; when more than one cause took people, the card says how many died in all
  • It warns when a town of yours stands open (nobody in it: anyone who marches on it has it), and when another people's open town is within reach of your armies
  • The last eight seasons' reports are kept in the save, and can be looked back on from the card (Past seasons) or from the Your people screen

Gaps

  • Seen in a browser only for a quiet season (one child born); a card with faces was checked by tests, not by eye
  • Men killed in a battle fought in person are counted when the battle is reckoned (with the army), not as the town's own dead
  • A season with nothing in it is not kept among the past seasons
  • Rivals' seasons are not reported, only the player's

Towns that keep their people

Working, rough

Every land has a real town: its people, its notable names and its stores, kept between seasons (design step 2).

  • Every land has a town with its people kept as counts (men, women, the old, and the children on the houses' records) and stores of food, wood, gold and stone; it is founded the first time it is asked after, so a campaign saved earlier still loads
  • A siege fought in person is fought by the town: the side holding the land turns out with exactly its people, old and young, and lives off its own stores in place of the usual stock; an attacking army's camp is as before
  • When the battle ends the town is what is left of it: who lived, the children on the record, what was gathered and spent; the campaign log says how many died and how many were carried off
  • A visit loads the town's people and stores and writes back what it leaves, as a siege does; a siege keeps the children born in it and those who came of age there
  • A town taken keeps the people who lived through it; one left empty is settled by four who followed the army
  • A battle settled from the map costs the town a share of its men (15% held, 30% taken) and, if it falls, half its stores
  • Notable people are kept by name on the town: a woman who has raised three children or more, and the soldier with the most kills who lived through a battle (in the chief town); a notable woman turns out by name in the next battle, and one who is lost stays on the record with when and how, and is written in the chronicle
  • The campaign screen shows each town's people, and for your own towns their stores and names; the nation's people and stores are shown as the sum of its towns'

Gaps

  • Between battles a town has births, children coming of age, old age and death by season (seasonfolk.ts, ageing.ts), but no harvest; nobody ages in a battle or a visit
  • An army is not yet the men of a town: its companies and an attacking camp's four villagers still come from nowhere, and the two clubmen who turn out with a defending town are extra; a man trained to arms in a siege is counted back as one of the town's men, but on the map he is a villager again and not a soldier of any army
  • Faith is still the nation's and not each town's
  • A town that turns out with all its people and has an army in it can begin a siege over its housing limit, and then nobody can be trained until houses are built or soldiers fall: found in a test, not yet decided whether that is right
  • People carried off are counted and then gone: they are not held anywhere on the map and cannot be got back after the battle
  • A notable woman is known only by children raised (not a farm kept or goods carried), and only women who have raised a child are counted by name; a veteran is kept in the chief town whichever town he came from, and is marked lost only when his whole army is
  • A field battle and a fight against marauders touch no town
  • Founding numbers (4 to 8 grown people, more in a capital) and the tolls are guesses; a town's age mix is drawn afresh each time it is loaded
  • Seen in a browser: the town panel, and one siege loading the town's people, stores and a named woman. No battle was played through to its end by hand, so the write-back is covered by tests only
  • Rivals' towns lose people by the same rules but rivals take no account of it

The map as a map

Working, rough

How the campaign map is drawn, and the weather and events that cross it.

  • Country matters: green country gives a farm 1 more, forest and the frozen north add 1 defence, marsh and desert weaken an army attacking into them (85% and 90%) and the open steppe strengthens it (110%), and land in desert or snow yields 1 less outside a capital; each land's panel says which applies
  • A border closed by a river or by mountains can be opened for good from either side by whoever holds the land: a bridge (5 supplies) or a road over the pass (8); it opens to everyone, is written in the chronicle, is kept in a saved game, and a rival with stores to spare builds one too
  • Settings has a choice for the campaign map (Automatic, Full, Plainer); Automatic draws the plainer map on a machine that draws in software or is small, and remembers a full map that took too long to appear; `?detail=low` or `high` in the address still overrides it
  • Towns and camps hold up close to: stone and plaster are laid in courses with mortar, timber runs in planks, roofs are rows of overlapping tile, tent cloth has a weave; houses have windows (some lamp-lit), lintels and timber framing; a capital has a paved square with a roofed well, market stalls under coloured awnings, barrels, a cart and garden trees; a hamlet has a well, a woodpile and a fenced garden. `?look=cap` (or `foe`, `tribe`, a land number) opens the map close in on a place
  • Every world has its own make, named in the side panel: one great continent, several continents parted by straits, two lands with a sea between, lands round an inner sea, or an archipelago; and its own temper (cold, hot, parched, rainy, dry or mild), with more or fewer mountains and lakes
  • The world is a wide sea nearly twice the old size, and land rises only round the towns: fifteen to thirty of forty possible places are taken, so coasts, bays and island chains fall differently every time; lands parted by water are joined by the shortest crossing so none is cut off
  • Sea lanes: where open water lies between two lands the crossing is a sea lane, marked on the sea in dashes from shore to shore, faint until a Harbour stands at one end; an army makes the crossing only from a land with a Harbour, and lands at four-fifths strength; a capital always keeps one way out on foot; a computer player shut in by the sea builds a Harbour first
  • Painted like an illustrated map: strong, distinct colours for each kind of country (olive downs, deep woods, gold desert, red rock in the heat, blue-grey rock in the cold, heather, woods in autumn orange), every ridge drawn in gold light and violet shade, a dark blue-green sea clouded like paint with a bright turquoise band of shallows and creeping surf round every coast, a ragged shoreline with small isles standing off it, and soft-edged cloud shadow
  • A living country in three dimensions, in the manner of a great war-map: every campaign grows its own world from its seed, with its own coastline, bays, lakes, mountain ranges and rivers
  • Six kinds of country: green country, deep forest, marshland, steppe, desert and the frozen north. Which end of the world is cold, and where the dry lands lie, differ from world to world
  • The ground is painted by what grows there, with rock and snow on the heights and sand at the shore; fir stands in the cold, broadleaf in green country, palm in the heat, stone in the dry
  • A land's country is named when you click it, and a battle there is fought in that country: snow, desert, steppe or green
  • Worlds are larger: 15 to 30 lands, where there were 12 to 24
  • Borders glow in the colour of whoever holds the land; the chosen land is lit, lands within reach are tinted, marauder country is darkened
  • The ground is painted at twice the fineness of the map, with a grain over it for close looking: meadow among darker grass, red sand in the desert, strata down the rock faces, a ragged snow line, pale shallows and surf at the shore
  • A quilt of small ploughed fields and pasture lies round every town in green country; rivers have a dark bank and a thread of light
  • Slopes are lit warm on the sunny side and cool in shade, shadows are sharper, and the corners of the view fall away into shade
  • The land is modelled twice as finely as before and the high country is broken into crag and gully
  • Ground detail is worked out point by point as the picture is drawn, so it stays sharp however close you come: grain in the soil at four scales, and bare banded rock wherever the land is steep
  • Fields, rivers and roads are drawn at four times the fineness of the map where the graphics card allows
  • Trees are rounded and lumpy like foliage, with ragged fir skirts, and shaded smoothly; soft light comes from all round the sky; the sea is glossy and catches the sun

Faith on the world map

Working, rough

Story and events

Working, rough

Named places, things that befall the whole world, tales put to you to decide, and a chronicle of it all.

  • 5 kinds of named place (a holy place, a meeting of roads, an old stronghold, iron in the ground, a house of learning), five to seven in every world, each with a name and one of the legends told of that place and no other, chosen by the seed and never in a capital
  • Whoever holds the land has the good of the place: piety, supplies, defence, cheaper companies or a cheaper age
  • 5 world events that befall every people at once: the Long Winter, Years of Plenty, the Season of Iron, a Comet, the Great Pestilence
  • 18 tales: every four turns a matter is brought before you with a pious answer and a worldly one, each with its price in supplies, standing, a rival's goodwill or a company of sellswords
  • Five of the tales are about a named place and are told only to whoever holds one of that kind: the keepers' peace, false weights at the fair, the last defenders under the keep, bound men at the forge's bellows, the closed book
  • Six tales are what an earlier answer led to, told at the next telling about the same land, rival and tribe: the exile you sheltered goes home or the one you sold is avenged; the tribe you fed asks to live under your rule (its land comes over without a blow) or the one you fleeced drives off your cattle; the sellswords want their winter pay; the shrine you built works a cure
  • No tale is told twice while others are still untold
  • A tale left unanswered settles itself when the turn ends; rivals answer tales of their own as their people lean
  • A chronicle keeps what will still matter at the end: the coming of the peoples, named places and capitals changing hands, peoples driven out, oaths broken, world events, your answers, and every battle you fought against a rival people, won or lost; it is read from the campaign screen with the places of note
  • When a campaign ends the last page is shown on the ending and in the chronicle: how it ended and how long it took, what your people had become, which places of note they held, and how you settled what was brought before you
  • Named places are marked on both maps and described on the land's panel; a world event is shown at the top of the campaign screen
  • Campaigns saved before this are given their places on loading

Gaps

  • Threads are one step long: an answer leads to one later tale and then stops, and nothing else that happens (a world event, a land lost, a leader's fall) starts one
  • Still no story with an ending of its own: the last page sums up, it does not conclude
  • Rival leaders do not value a named place, plan for a world event or speak of either
  • The tales of named places and the threads were read through and driven by test; none was met in a campaign played by hand, so how often they come up is unknown
  • The last page was seen in a browser for a campaign won by conquest only; a defeat and a win by dominion were checked by test alone
  • Battles between rivals are not in the chronicle unless a capital or a named place changed hands
  • Places have no look of their own on the 3D map: a seal only (the 3D map could not be drawn on the machine this was built on)
  • The numbers are first guesses and no campaign has been played with them

Day and night

Working, rough

The hours of the day, and what the dark changes.

  • A day is five minutes, the last three tenths of it night
  • Settings (on the main menu and behind the help button) set the length of a day to three, five or ten minutes, and switch night off: with it off the hours run from dawn to dusk and back, so the light never jumps and never goes, and the rule that units see less in the dark never applies
  • There is a sky: a dome from the mist at the horizon up to blue or to the dark, the sun standing in it where the light comes from (gold when low, near white when high, with the sky brightest round it), a moon with its grey seas by night, and 650 stars that are the same every night; weather dims them
  • The main menu's camera now sits low and looks into the sunrise, so the sky and the morning sun are in the picture behind the town
  • In a match the O key brings the camera down to stand low and turns it to face the sun, or the moon by night, so the sky, the sunset and the stars are seen in play; [ and ] turn the view, the wheel raises and drops the eyes, and O goes back. The bottom panel is put away while the view is down
  • By night the six flames nearest the middle of the view cast real light: walls, roofs, people and trees beside a lamp or a torch are lit on the side that faces it
  • The leader and about one soldier in three carry a torch by night, held up in the left hand (`src/client/torch.ts`): a wooden torch with a pitch head, the arm raised over whatever the figure is doing, the torch upright however the hand turns, and the flame and its light set on the torch's tip, with a glow on the ground at their feet; riders carry one too; it comes down by day and goes out when its bearer falls
  • A setting turns the real light off for a slow machine; the lamps and torches still show their glow
  • The sun crosses the sky from dawn to dusk: low and red at either end with long shadows, high and white at midday; by night a dim blue moonlight
  • Buildings show a lamp by the door and throw a pool of warm light by night, each guttering on its own
  • The top bar always names the hour: dawn, morning, midday, afternoon, dusk, night
  • In the dark units see two tiles less, so they are slower to notice an enemy and less of the map is lit up; buildings see as far as ever
  • Work camps shut their captives in at night, and that is when captives slip away or rise
  • Fireflies come out, and hearth smoke thickens

Gaps

  • The computer player takes no account of the dark: it does not attack by night on purpose
  • Looking out is a view for looking, not for playing: orders can still be given to what is in sight, but the panel and the small map are put away, and the view does not follow the sun as it moves. At midday the sun is too high to be in it until the eyes are raised
  • From low down, land not yet known lies as a flat bank of mist of the sky's colour, and it is drawn over the feet of whatever stands behind it
  • The sun and moon are drawn lower in the sky than their light falls from, so that they can be seen at all
  • Torches in the hand were seen in one still of a night scene, standing: foot soldiers and a rider. Not seen: walking, fighting, a bearer falling, or the change at dusk and dawn
  • Archers, crossbowmen and gunners carry no torch, since both hands are taken, so fewer flames show than one in three; a shield on the torch arm rides up with it; the glow on the ground sits a fixed drop below the flame, a little high under a raised torch
  • Villagers, ships and beasts carry none; war beasts and engines with no person drawn keep the flame at the shoulder
  • Only six flames cast real light at once, the nearest to the middle of the view; the rest only glow. Light does not cast shadows
  • What the real light costs was measured only on a slow software renderer, where it was lost in the noise in one run (about 1.3 to 1.5 seconds a frame either way) and a fifth slower in another. On a real graphics card it is unmeasured
  • Changing the length of a day in the middle of a battle moves the hour at once, since the hour is worked out from the time played. The settings are the player's and are not part of a saved game
  • With night off the sun goes back the way it came after dusk
  • Seen in a browser: the dawn sky and sun on the main menu, lamps and torches lighting a town at night with the setting on and off, the three settings and that they reach the game, and looking out in a match at dusk, at night (moon and stars), at dawn and at midday. Not seen: turning the view by hand and coming back to the game's own view (only that the camera got there), a storm over the sky, a long or short day played through, or a night with night switched off
  • Nothing else changes by night: villagers work on, wolves are no bolder

Marauders

Working, rough

A band that belongs to no people: it carries off women, grows by them, and can be broken.

  • Four minutes into a skirmish or a siege, marauders make camp in the wilds, as far from every town as the land allows
  • They come out in parties to carry off women from the nearest people, one to a man, and lead them back to the camp
  • Every woman held bears the band children: the camp raises a new marauder about every two minutes for each woman it holds, up to 28
  • Finding the camp tells you how many of your women are held there; selecting it names them and how many children each has borne
  • Destroy the camp and everyone held goes free and comes home
  • A woman held there can be got back for ten minutes from being seized; after that she is dead, and burning the camp brings no one back. One freed in time comes home older by the years it cost her, with the children she bore there
  • A woman who was held there bears no more children, but works a quarter harder than the rest; one rescued on the road, before she reached the camp, is unharmed
  • On the campaign map marauders take root in a tribal land far from any capital, grow every turn, carry off women from the towns next to them (real people, held at the camp for four seasons: see Threats of men on the map), and overrun a tribe's land next door when strong enough
  • Taking a marauder land breaks that camp: the women go home and the victor gains 4 supplies
  • Marauder land is washed black on the map and flies a black banner over a ring of stakes
  • `?marauders=0` leaves them out of a battle
  • A computer player learns where the camp is by losing a woman to it or by coming in sight of it, then marches on it once its army outweighs the band (sooner if its own women are held), and comes home when it is burnt
  • Its idle soldiers run down a raiding party that comes near its town: a marauder killed drops the woman he leads
  • They keep pace with the age: for each age the most advanced people has reached, a marauder has 30% more health and strikes 25% harder, and the camp's stockade is 30% stronger
  • A burnt camp is not the end: if any of the band are alive when it falls they make camp again five minutes later, on new ground at least 20 tiles from the ashes, and fresh men join them. Kill every last one first and the band is finished for the battle; every people is told which it is

Gaps

  • The computer player hunts the camp but does not scout for it, and does not hide its women when a party comes; the rules for both (scoutSpots, partyNear and hideWomen in src/sim/marauders.ts) are written and tested but the computer does not call them yet
  • The computer comes home once the camp is burnt and does not hunt the survivors, so against computer players the band will usually come back
  • A band whose camp has been burnt stands where it is until the new camp is made: it does not flee or hide
  • Hunting was measured in one game: the camp fell at 16 minutes, not 30, and held 2 of that player's women at most, not 14
  • On the campaign map rival leaders ignore the marauders altogether
  • A player cannot run such a camp: marauders are always a third party
  • Strength by age and the return after a burning were tried in tests only: the numbers are guesses, and a late-age band has not been played against
  • A captive in the marauders' camp cannot be let out by soldiers at the gate as one in a Work Camp can: it must still be burnt
  • Tried in two games between computer players and in still pictures; never played against

People's records

Working, rough

Each villager is someone, with a record of what has happened to them.

  • Notable people are told on sight and by name (`src/sim/notable.ts`, `src/client/notables.ts`): a soldier who has felled three or more and the mother of a house of renown carry gold stars over their figure (one to three), with their name beside it close in; their panel says what they are known for; and the Town Center's panel lists the town's notable people, leader first. The list takes additions from other modules (`extra`), which is where those remembered from earlier seasons can be plugged in
  • A woman is known by her home's output as well as the children it raised (notableweight.ts): whatever its man and woman carry in to the stores is counted to the house, food from a field half again for a farm well kept, and every 400 is a rank of renown in the battle and of standing on the town's record, which keeps the most it has done from season to season and gives it back to her house when the town turns out
  • A veteran is a notable person of the town they came from when their army knows it (`Champion.town`), else of the chief town
  • A loss weighs beyond the record: a town grieves its known people for four seasons, more for the famous, and the nation's standing dips by the square of their fame; the town's people wear grief in their portraits and its panel says whom it mourns
  • Villagers have a name, a family, an age, and up to two traits
  • A record is kept for each: children had at home, when and by whom they were carried off, children borne in captivity, when they were freed or taken into another tribe
  • The record is shown on the villager's panel like the entries on a form, with the time of each
  • The record goes with them through captivity and back

Gaps

  • Losing a known person is weighed (notableweight.ts) but nothing applies it yet: the town's grief and the nation's dip in standing are numbers for drift.ts to read, and only the town's face (a grieving portrait and a line on its panel) shows them
  • Veterans belong to the town they came from only once an army knows its town: until armies are towns' (another session's work), every veteran is still of the chief town
  • The stars and name over a veteran were seen in a real game; a matron's mark, the panel line and the Town Center's list were not seen in a browser
  • The three buttons whose pictures showed children (resume births, Fertility Rites, Extended Family) show a plain symbol: new pictures need art generation, which was not done
  • Fathers' children are not counted, only mothers'
  • Children are not linked to their mother by name: the record is a count
  • Soldiers have a count of kills on their panel, but no battles survived and no story
  • Nothing is recorded for work done, buildings raised or wounds taken
  • Records do not carry over between battles of a campaign

Finding the way

Working, rough

How units get from one place to another.

  • Routes go round buildings, water, rock and woods, prefer roads and avoid bad ground
  • A unit that is built over, or caught inside a footprint, walks straight out to open ground and carries on
  • A goal that cannot be reached is recognised at once, and the unit goes as near as it can without searching the whole map
  • A resource nobody can get to is left alone for a minute, so villagers are not sent after it one by one
  • Measured in trial games between computer players: units with somewhere to go stand stuck about a quarter of one percent of the time (it was up to 3.7%)

Gaps

  • Units still pass through one another on the march and are only nudged apart when they stop
  • No formations: a group ordered to a point arrives in a loose square
  • A few villagers still wait at a crowded mine
  • Only tried with computer players; never watched by a person for odd routes

Computer opponent

Working, rough

Rivals that build, fight and sail.

  • Builds a town, advances, attacks and defends
  • Raises its army out of its own people now that a soldier costs a villager: it sends nobody to arms, to a ship's crew or to the Slavers while it has no more than 8, 12, 16, 20 or 22 villagers at work (by age; half that when attacked, and half for a fishing boat, which still feeds the town), and trains villagers back up to its usual number (in a skirmish or mission: in the campaign it cannot, so it houses every couple instead and sends unmarried men to arms first)
  • A people is beaten when its last Town Center has fallen and it has not begun another within a minute and a half (twenty seconds if it has no soldier left); a war band that has never settled is beaten as before
  • In a town with no room left near its Town Center it builds further out: it used to try the same site for ever, and a people starved beside a full treasury for want of a market it had nowhere to put. It does so only after thirty tries close in: looking far at once spread a young town out and lost its people to marauders
  • Goes for the Town Center once the defenders are down to their last two soldiers, and for one being raised at once; not before, which in trial games threw armies away
  • Builds a navy, raids by sea, lands armies
  • Staffs its Church and Tavern, hires mercenaries
  • Turns idle wealth into soldiers: the richer the treasury, the larger the army it keeps
  • Does not march into an army it cannot beat: it waits to outweigh the defenders, though not for ever
  • Calls a failed attack home instead of dying to the last man, and sends reinforcements up together, not one by one
  • In nine trial games between settled computer players every one ended with a winner, in 26 to 39 minutes (before, one in five ended inside 25)
  • Raiders: while the nearest rival has next to no soldiers the whole band goes for its villagers; later a few slip out at a time, keeping clear of towers and town centres, and a raider who is badly hurt comes home
  • A raider fights armed men before he takes villagers, and does not stand idle beside one he cannot take
  • Builds a market first when gold lies idle and food or wood has run out, and buys more at once the more gold it holds
  • Does not raise yesterday's soldiers once better ones can be trained, unless gold has run out
  • How many men make an army worth sending is set by the age, not the treasury: a rich player used to wait for an army it could never raise
  • Keeps no more than two priests or healers: pious peoples used to fill their armies with them
  • A long war makes it bolder: from the twenty-fifth minute it asks for a smaller margin, and after half an hour an attack is fought to the finish with every man sent up as he is raised
  • Builds more barracks, ranges and stables when its treasury is full, and sells a great heap of one thing faster

Gaps

  • Since a soldier costs a person, towns and armies are smaller and wars a little longer: in forty-eight trial wars (two sets of twenty-four, old and new on one tree) the mean went from 30.6 to 31.3 minutes, wars past 35 minutes from 9 to 13, undecided at 45 from 1 to 2; 24 were shorter and 23 longer, and the winner changed in 26. At the 20th minute a people has 29 villagers and 14 soldiers where it had 36 and 19; the largest army it ever fields is 22.9, from 27.0, and its most villagers 35.8, from 43.1. Fewer die (75 a people, from 112). None of this was tuned
  • The figures in the next entry are from before a soldier cost a person. Wars between evenly matched computer players take about half an hour, and most of that is peace: between settled peoples there are one to three attacks in a whole war, the first usually between the 22nd and 30th minute; the side that marches at even odds loses its army to the town's defences and the counter-attack ends the war inside five minutes. In forty-eight trial games (two sets of twenty-four, each run old and new on one tree) the mean is 29.4 minutes, six ran past 35 and one was undecided at 45; before the crowded-town fix it was 30.3, thirteen and one. Between settled peoples alone the mean went from 31.2 to 28.5. Twenty-six wars were shorter and twenty longer: it is the long wars that are fewer, not every war that is shorter
  • Tried and dropped, because they did not hold up on a second set of games: not letting a new age restart the wait before attacking; striking whenever the enemy is clearly the weaker; making late reinforcements gather before going up
  • In those sixteen games raiders won 4 and settled peoples 8; sixteen games is too few to call that settled
  • The Norse were the weaker raiders; their food bonus now covers berries and fish as well as game, and in eight trial games they won 4, lost 3 and one was undecided (before: 1, 5 and 2). Eight games is a small sample
  • Towns still grow to 80 or 100 buildings; they no longer all have to fall, but that was never much of the length: under the old rule the loser's Town Center fell about two and a half minutes before the end
  • After half an hour an attack is fought to the finish and every new soldier goes up alone; in one trial war that fed eight soldiers a minute into the enemy for a quarter of an hour. Making them gather first changed nothing measurable once the crowded-town fix was in, so it was left
  • The clock on a people with no Town Center was seen in a browser running for the player and one rival together and counting down; the 20-second and 300-second cases, an ally's clock and the clock stopping when a hall is begun were not seen, and it is drawn from the same state the rule keeps
  • A losing computer player can begin another Town Center to stay in the war: looked for in twenty-four trial games, it happened once and bought under a minute. A person with stores could do it for longer; nothing stops that
  • A leader's temperament changes four things only: when it attacks, army size, walls, and church or tavern
  • Has never been played against by a person

  • The Mountain King: English in the snow with two Prophets against a Japanese keep behind old stone that one Earthquake breaches; each of Earthquake, Plague, and Blight or fire is counted as it falls on him

Gaps

  • The five newer missions were played by test only (each objective done by command and checked), never by hand: how long they take and how hard they are is unknown; the two newest were also loaded in a browser, where they opened without error in the right country

Working, rough

Helping a new player start.

  • A people with no Town Center has its time left shown on the screen while it runs, under the top bar: your own in red, any other people's beneath it, with the last twenty seconds beating
  • A first-steps guide in every battle and a controls sheet
  • The guide takes up faith once the Bronze Age is reached (build a Church or a Tavern and put villagers in it), and on a map with water the Dock and its boats while the town is still small
  • Missions that teach by doing: a first town, church and tavern, ships, playing as raiders
  • The campaign has first orders of its own on a slip over the map, seven of them, each marked done by what the campaign shows the player has done: raise a company, build, take a neighbouring land, end the turn, speak to a rival, advance an age, and how the world is won, with that world's own numbers

Look and sound 7

3D world

Working, rough

The battlefield and its light.

  • Painted ground, scattered grass and stones
  • Shores run in smooth curves, not tile steps: the land falls away into the bed along a rounded line and the water thins to a soft edge of surf just short of it
  • Water takes its colour from the country: bright and clear in the desert, green over a temperate shelf, grey and cold in the north; rain rings its surface and the moon glints on it at night
  • Water with a moving surface of swell and chop: it mirrors the sky at a low angle and shows its depth looking down, catches the sun in points of glitter, shelves over several tiles from clear turquoise shallows with a net of light on the bed to deep blue, sends line after line of surf in to the beach, washes up and back along the sand, greys under weather and darkens at night
  • Hills that rise and fall and mountain ranges with snow on the peaks
  • Low warm sunlight, soft shadows, haze and drifting cloud shadows
  • A drawn finish: banded shade with an ink outline, heavy on buildings and fine on people
  • Weather that comes and goes: rain, snowfall in the north, dust in the desert

Gaps

  • The computer opponent does not plan around the weather
  • No seasons, no thunder, no sound of rain
  • Water mirrors a sky with drifting cloud but not the land or ships; ships ride the long swell but not the small chop
  • The water costs more to draw than anything else on the ground and has not been measured on a phone
  • Rain rings on water and ships riding the swell run but have not been seen
  • How fast it runs on a phone or in a big battle has not been measured

Buildings

Working, rough

What a town is made of.

  • The buildings that answer the systems have models of their own in the house style (src/client/civic.ts), by the nation's walls and roof at its age and the holy and low leanings; each replaces its stand-in (standins.ts) as it reaches the game. In the game so far: the Hall of Judgement (a columned porch on steps, the scales over the door, a tower behind), the Granary (on mushroom stones), the Mill (a horse mill, then a tower mill with sails), the Well, the Infirmary (its physic garden, the sign of healing by people), the Watch House (lookout, lantern, stocks), the Noble's Manor (behind its courtyard wall) and the Monastery (round its cloister, its people's sign on the tower). Drawn and waiting for their buildings: Fortress, Inn; and a Bathhouse, an Almshouse and the Convent's look, should they be wanted
  • Every building shaped by hand, with a ruined church for a decadent town: pitched roofs with eaves, timber framing, tiles and thatch, stonework, round towers, props
  • A building going up rises from its footings inside a scaffold of poles; roofs differ a shade from house to house
  • Walls join up: each piece reaches toward its neighbours, corners and ends get a heavier post, gates turn to stand across the wall
  • Each nation keeps its own roofs (domes, pagodas, flat roofs, tents) and colours by age
  • Church and tavern drawn bright, plain, ruined or gaudy by the town's standing
  • Each nation's people look like where they come from: fair in the north and west of Europe, olive round the Mediterranean, tan to brown in the Near East and on the Nile, light tan with black hair on the steppe and in the Far East; within a people the shades are close, and a captive keeps the look of the people they were taken from
  • People differ from one another: hair worn short, long, knotted or balding, beards and moustaches on some men, and faces with brows, a nose, a mouth and ears
  • What flies has its own shape and ending: fletched arrows and bolts point first, stones and boulders tumbling, cannon balls nearly flat; they land in dust, a grey puff or a splash, and a boulder shakes the ground
  • An engine or ship below half strength burns and smokes, worse as she nears her end; a troop ship shows up to six of those aboard
  • A ship that is sunk breaks up: masts, yards and sails come down around her, her crew go into the water, and the hull rolls over and settles by the stern in foam; a wrecked engine throws its crew clear
  • Siege engines are built as machines with their working parts and a crew: a catapult with its skein, stop bar and winch; a ram hung on chains under a roof of hides; a bolt-thrower with spring frame and slide; a trebuchet with trestles, counterweight box and sling; a banded great gun on a field carriage; a two-tier organ gun behind a mantlet
  • Four ships: a fishing boat with a lateen sail, net and catch; a galley with a ram, shields along the rail and five oars a side; a troop cog with castles and cargo; a warship with bowsprit, two masts with topsails and guns run out
  • Clothes have collars, cuffs, stitched hems, belt buckles and lacing; boots have soles and turned cuffs; hands have thumbs
  • Village women dress as their town lives: scarves where it is pious, laced bodices worn low where it is decadent, lower still at the extreme
  • In a worldly or decadent town a tavern with both maids and patrons almost never has a child (tens of minutes apart at most), and then one no household owns to; hearts rise from houses where a couple is at home, and from nowhere else
  • A day is five minutes, its last three tenths night: the light goes blue and dim and the top bar says so
  • The work camp is a four-tile compound of two rooms: a workroom where the women are kept at the querns by day, and a bunkhouse the men go out from to break stone and back into; at dusk all are shut into the bunkhouse and a lamp shows
  • Slavers can be posted at a camp as guards (up to four); each makes its men count a quarter more toward the town's work
  • Holding people has a cost: each guard watches three captives; by night an unwatched one slips away about once a night and runs for home, and a camp with four or more men unwatched may rise, burn and lose half of all it holds at a stroke
  • A Decadent town is dying out and grows by what it takes: its households have children less than half as often and half the births that are due do not come, villagers take twice as long to raise at the Town Center, and its Work Camp holds twelve
  • A villager taken by a Slaver is seen: stripped to a loincloth (a woman keeps a band across the chest), barefoot, roped at the neck and wrists, led behind the Slaver and then held in the work camp yard, the men at labour
  • A pious town keeps flowers at its doors. No cast-off clothes are drawn anywhere: they were removed from the tavern and from the houses of a Decadent town

Gaps

  • Walls have not been checked on sloping ground or beside towers
  • One wonder for every nation, differing only in its roof
  • Houses come in four shapes with varied yards (woodpile, barrel, vegetable patch, washing line, well); the storehouse and barracks have two; the steppe keeps its houses, town centre, church, tavern, storehouse, barracks, smithy, stable, range and workshop under round felt tents (its market, tower and dock are still walled); every other building still has one shape
  • Nations share one set of building shapes under their own roofs

Damage and death

Working, rough

What fighting does to people and buildings.

  • Buildings shed pieces as they weaken and fall apart when destroyed

  • Wounds show on the figure in two stages, at two thirds and one third strength, and again in death: cuts and blood painted on the body and clothes

  • Animals show no wounds
  • Riders do not fall from their horses

  • The switch for blood is an address option, not a setting in the menu
