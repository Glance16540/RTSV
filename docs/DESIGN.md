# Rise and Ruin: the design every session works from

Set by the owner on 14 November 2026. Where this page and the code disagree, the code is behind:
this page says where the game is going. Where it and `scripts/systems.mjs` disagree about what is
true *today*, the board is right. Change this page only on the owner's word.

## What the game is

A nation and its people, lived over generations. The world map is the game. A real-time battle is
not a separate mode: it is the close view of one town in that nation, and everything that happens
in it goes home. The work is no longer written story; it is systems deep enough that stories fall
out of them by chance: pirates on the coast take the women and burn the farms, and the nation is
poorer for a generation.

## Decided

**Time.** A turn on the map is a season, four to a year. A child is grown at 18 (the owner's word, 18 November 2026: every child comes of age at eighteen, whatever the age or people; a child's years run faster to make up for it, so that eighteen is reached in the fifteen years of seasons it took before) and old at
about 60. Entering a town plays a fixed stretch of that season in real time. Every town the player
does not visit lives the same season by the same rules, run coarsely.

A person ages only on the map, a season at a time. Nobody grows older inside a battle or a visit,
and nobody dies of age there: in a town people die only by violence, sickness, fire or hunger.
Each person's age is kept on the town's record, so a town loads with the same people it left with.

**Age belongs to the nation.** It advances only on the map. Nobody ages up inside a town; a town is
in whatever age its nation is in.

**Towns persist.** A land has a real town: its people, buildings and stores, loaded when the player
enters and saved when they leave. The people on screen are the population: ten women taken is ten
women gone. From the map the player may enter any of their towns at any time to train, build and
keep order.

**No auto-resolve.** A town the player is not in defends itself with what it has: walls, guards,
people, its state. It may hold, be sacked, be taken or be destroyed. The player is given a report
of the defence: who was killed, who was taken, and which way the town's morals swayed.

**A soldier costs a person.** Training takes a villager as well as the usual resources, and the
training time is what turns him into the unit chosen. No man comes from nowhere. Population is the
real resource: an army is paid for in births and years.

**People come only by birth.** In the campaign (a battle or a visit) no villager is bought at the
Town Center: a town's people are those it has and those its housed couples raise. Skirmish keeps
villagers for hire as it was.

Births happen in both places and are one record: on the map a town's couples raise children season by
season, coarsely; inside a battle or a visit they are born faster, in real time. Either way the people
born are the town's and persist: a town brings its real people into a visit or a siege, and what is
left of them, with everyone born there, goes back to it afterwards.

**Men and women.** Soldiers come from the men. Women may serve, in healing and support; a woman
who fights does so with a much lower attack. Women tend the farms, raise the children, work,
produce, and carry more than a man. War takes the men and raiders take the women, and those are
two different wounds.

**Children are never shown.** A child is written in the record of the house it is born to (name,
parents, age, what it may inherit) and is not seen, does not leave the house and cannot be struck,
taken or harmed until it comes of age, when it steps out as a grown villager. No violence is ever
done to a child, on screen or in the rules.
A child whose town is left with no grown people is taken in by the nearest town of its own people,
with the record of the house it belongs to. If its people has no town left, the nearest
other people takes it in and it grows up as one of theirs.

**A man at war is still of his house.** A man who leaves to fight stays a member of his household:
what he earns and wins in war counts to the house's output and renown as his work at home did, and
he comes home to it. His absence is felt (the house has one pair of hands fewer for the fields) but
he is not lost to it until he dies or is taken.
If his house falls while he is away, its record falls with it: he keeps his family's name but belongs
to no house until he is home, and what he wins meanwhile counts to none. An unmarried son's deeds count
to the house he was born in while it stands; he comes home without a roof and may found his own.

**Icons tell status.** The picture beside a person is the main way the player reads them, so it
shows their people (each nation drawn in its own dress, skin and arms) and their town's standing
(devout, ordinary, worldly, decadent: the women plainly more alluring and the men plainly crueller
as a town falls). A person's condition has its own mark as well: wounded, maimed, captive, led
away, freed, sick, starving, grieving, old, veteran, gone to war, deserter, in revolt, drunk.
Icons are the only way the game shows feeling. Each person keeps one body for life (full-figured or slender, broad or lean) and their face shows how they are: content, afraid, grieving or angry.

**Notable people.** Most villagers are ordinary. Some earn a name the nation remembers: a soldier
who rises through veterancy, and a woman whose home produces most (many children raised, a farm
well kept, much carried in). Their loss carries weight for the nation, not only for the town.

**Captivity.** People taken are held somewhere real on the map and can be got back for a time.
Captivity shortens a life: a captive man loses about twenty years, and a captive woman does not
long outlive her use to her captors. What the captor gains depends on who he is. A rival nation
that assimilates gains population outright. Slavers gain output: the captives' labour and the
children born in captivity raise what the holder produces. What is shown of it is the place and the condition: the stockade, the quarters, the
captives' dress, the work they are put to, the guards. What is done to a captive woman is told by
what follows from it (a shorter life, children born in captivity), and is not shown.
Nothing is drawn to suggest it, not even by a sign.
A captive of slavers or marauders is drawn in a ragged loincloth (a woman with a thick band covering her chest), hands tied behind the back with rope (no chains), dishevelled and worn down; a captive of a rival nation stays clothed, bound and bowed.

Children born in captivity: where the captor is a slaver or a marauder, they are the mother's and
her people's, and come home with her if she is got back. Where the captor is a rival nation that
assimilates, they are that nation's.

**Acts of God.** Famine, plague, fire and the like come at random. They fall harder on the
decadent than on the holy, but spare nobody: a Righteous town has them at about a third of the
rate and severity, and very rarely is struck hard.

**Threats of men never stop.** Raiders, highwaymen, slavers and pirates live on the map, choose
their marks and strike. There is always order to keep and something to fight.

**Drift.** Villagers do as they are set to while fed and safe. As more of them struggle, more turn
to what is worse for them: scarce food sends women to the taverns and men to the mines, and the
walls are left thin. Where a town is well kept the holy way is the more attractive and people
drift toward it instead.

**Decrees.** The leader rules by decree as well as by building. A church draws people back, guards
keep order by force, food removes the cause, closing the tavern works and angers a decadent town.
A decree can also go the other way: ordering the women into the taverns raises gold and lowers the
town's moral standing.

**The tavern as a town falls.** As a town grows less moral its tavern women come to sell more than
drink. This is shown by the house and not by any act: the tavern's look and lights, its hours, how
its women are dressed, the gold it brings in, and the town's standing going down.

**A visit.** Entering a town plays about ten minutes of its season; the player may leave sooner.
Any number of towns may be visited in a season, each once.

**A town left to itself** carries on with the last orders it was given (what to build, what to
train, where its people work), and a governor fills the gaps sensibly.

**Stores.** The four resources replace the map's supplies. Every town has its own stores of food,
wood, gold and stone, and the nation's stores on the map are the sum of its towns'. Towns send
food, goods and people to one another by road, a season on the way, and a convoy can be robbed.

**Armies belong to towns.** An army is the men of the town that raised it, and its dead are that
town's dead. A veteran who comes home is one of its notable people.

**A dead town.** A town with nobody left in it stands open: any rival nation or band of marauders that
comes for it can take it over (the owner's word, 15 November 2026). It does not simply go back to the wild.
A people that outlives every rival, however it came about, has won: being the last people alive is a
victory (the owner's word, 15 November 2026).
A world where no people has been able to take land for a long stretch (about twenty years) while bands
hold much of the map ends in ruin: no winner, and the chronicle closes the age (the owner's word, 15
November 2026).

**A town taken.** Its people stay and become the taker's over time: resentful at first and likely
to rise, sooner settled where they are ruled well.

**Rivals live by the same rules.** A rival nation has real towns, loses real people, and can be
bled by raids as the player can.

**Faith is a town's.** Each town stands where it stands between piety and decadence, and towns of
one nation may stand at different levels. The nation's standing is made from its towns': their
average, so that a nation's creed does not swing with every town won or lost. A nation also pulls its
towns toward its own standing, so they may drift apart only so far.

**The leader is one person in one place.** He moves about the map, is in a town or with an army,
and steadies the town he is in. He can call armies to meet him and move with them as one.

**Grief.** A home that loses its mother or its sons works more slowly for a time. It tells most in
a town of middling morals, and less in a holy or a decadent one.

**Relationships** (the owner's word, 15 November 2026). Taking land is not enough to bring a player
back; people are. The leader is a person among people, and what happens to those close to them matters
as much as what happens to the nation.

- *The leader's own household.* The leader has a spouse, and children who have a mother or a father,
  with names, ages and faces. They can be threatened, married off, lost or taken, like anyone.
- *The inner circle.* A few named people stand closest to the leader: a right hand, a steward, a
  marshal, a priest or a confidant. Each has a name, a face, a mood and a loyalty that can turn.
- *Nobles.* Some people rise into a noble class through what they did or the post they were given:
  a veteran captain, a governor, a matron of a great house. Nobles have a say in decrees. A decree
  pleases some and angers others, and their moods tell on the towns: an angry lord's town is slacker,
  readier to drift and less watchful against raiders while the leader is away.
- *Stories inside the story.* Personal matters come up by chance, out of the systems already running,
  and play out over seasons: a rival king who wants the leader dead and their spouse for his own; a
  suitor for the leader's daughter; a noble plotting to take the throne; a thief who keeps robbing the
  same houses; a killer in a town whom the leader's people must find. Each is told through named people
  and their faces, has a choice or a search in it, and leaves a mark on the record whatever comes of it.
  None touches a child, and nothing is shown of what is done to a captive.
- *Several campaigns.* A player can keep more than one world, each with its own history, and go back to
  any of them.

**Meeting them face to face** (the owner's word, 16 November 2026). When the player meets someone on the
map, the people they meet are shown as people. When an army runs into a band of marauders, or a decision
comes up (a suitor, a rival king's envoy, a plotter found, a thief brought for judgement), a window opens
over the map. It shows the ones met as living 3D figures, the band's leader at the front with some of
his men, or the person asking, dressed and armed as they are. The choices sit beside them. The map stays
behind it, and nothing else is changed by it. The same rules hold as everywhere: no child is shown, and
nothing suggestive is shown of a captive.

**Buildings and research that answer the systems** (the owner's word, 16 November 2026). What a player
builds and learns should answer what happens to their people: hunger, plague, crime, plots, nobles,
roads and faith, and not only make soldiers hit harder. Each new building or study has a job in a system
that already runs, works on the map as well as in a town, and is kept on the town's record.

- *Food:* a Granary keeps food against a failed harvest, so a town goes hungry later and drifts less in
  famine. A Mill makes farms yield more. Crop Rotation means fewer failed harvests; Salting and Smoking
  keep food longer.
- *Health:* a Well (or Bathhouse) slows plague in a crowded town. An Infirmary (or Almshouse) tends the
  sick and the grieving. Quarantine keeps plague from crossing between towns; Midwifery makes births
  safer; Physicians improves on Herbalism.
- *Rule and law:* a Hall of Judgement (the leader's seat) is where thieves and plotters are judged and
  nobles gather; a decree given there has its full strength. A Noble's Manor in a large town raises its
  lord's loyalty but gives him more say. Couriers strengthens decrees sent by messenger; Stewardship makes
  a better governor; Written Law eases unrest and a taken town's resentment; Oaths of Fealty steadies
  nobles' loyalty; Spymasters finds plots sooner.
- *Order and roads:* a Fortress, which the governor already raises on the map, stands in the town as a
  stronghold against bands. A Watch House catches thieves sooner and finds a killer's clues faster. An
  Inn on a road keeps convoys safer from highwaymen. Town Watch, Heralds (raid news a season sooner),
  Paved Roads and Escorts.
- *Faith:* a Monastery or Convent, the Righteous town's great house, as the Work Camp is the Decadent
  one's. Feast Days and Pilgrimage.
- *Each people its own:* every nation has one or two studies of its own that fit what it is (Franks
  their families, Babylonians their law, Malians their trade, Kushites their bows, Romans their walls).

**The food decree buys grain abroad** (the owner's word, 17 November 2026). Sending food between a
people's own towns only moves a lean season from one larder to the next. The decree that answers a
hungry town brings in new food from outside the nation, paid for in its gold, so that it ends hunger
rather than shifting it.

**Settlers found new towns, and bands come from the far wilds** (the owner's word, 17 November 2026).
A people may send settlers, two couples and their children, from one of its towns to an empty land
beside its own, where they found a small new town of that people without a war; rivals may do the
same. And some bands are born in the wild lands at the map's edge and march in from afar toward rich
or weak towns, so that a people can see them coming.
Some lands begin wild: a share of the far and poor lands (snow, desert, the map's edge) start with no
tribe and no town. They are where settlers go and where the wild bands arise.
Snow and desert wild lands are true wilderness: they can never be settled, so a rim of wild country
lasts all campaign and bands keep arising there to march in from afar.

**A town's own life moves its faith** (the owner's word, 17 November 2026). Hunger, raids and plague
draw a town toward decadence; a long peace with full stores draws it toward piety; so one town of a
nation can stand apart from the rest by what it has lived through.
A town founded by settlers begins partly apart from its nation's faith: those who leave are not quite
like those who stay.

**Towns big enough to raise armies** (the owner's word, 17 November 2026). So that a soldier truly
costs a person, towns begin and grow large enough to fill the companies a people fields: a company is
raised only from the men of its people's towns, never hired from nowhere, and armies stay the size they
are. Towns are about four times as large as they were (a new capital some two dozen men, a grown
town a hundred), and a company costs one man of the town and his following. A bigger town has more
houses, not fuller ones: a house holds five people on the map as in a battle.

**A garrison pays for its guard** (the owner's word, 17 November 2026). A raid on a town is fought out, the band against the town's men, works and walls and any army standing there; and an army standing guard in a raided town loses companies when it is pressed, as in any battle, each a man of his town.

**Every town its own faith** (the owner's word, 17 November 2026). All towns differ in faith, even those built alike, and together they make the nation's faith. A town leans its own way by its own life as well as by its church or tavern; the nation's standing is what its towns come to together.

**A rim of true wilderness** (the owner's word, 17 November 2026). The map's edge is ringed with true wilderness, snow and desert that nobody can settle or farm, so a rim of wild country lasts all campaign and bands can be born there and march in from afar where a people can see them coming. It takes no farmland from anyone.

**A dip does not undo dominion** (the owner's word, 17 November 2026). A people that holds the share for dominion counts its turns toward the ten; a season under the share takes two turns off the count, not all of it, so one town rising after nine turns does not throw away a won war.

**One rule for who defends a town** (the owner's word, 18 November 2026). A town struck by a band turns out as it does against a nation: its men as militia bands, any army there beside them, its walls and towers striking and sheltering. A large town holds against a band somewhat less often than when its every man was counted, and that is accepted, since it now holds by the same rule whoever comes.

**Caravans on the roads** (the owner's word, 18 November 2026). A market's trade is carried by caravans that can be seen on the roads between towns, and a band can fall on one, as it can on an army. What a market earns comes by the caravans that arrive, not by rule alone.

**Claimants to the throne** (the owner's word, 18 November 2026). A noble whose faith is not the leader's may plot to overthrow the leader. Such a noble may take the leader's wife into his house, and a child of theirs may grow up to claim the throne as the noble's bastard heir. So too a leader's wife carried off and held: a child she bears there may, grown, come seeking the throne. A claimant raises a rebellion among those who will follow him. As ever, nothing of it is shown or told beyond the plain fact (a wife taken, a child born, a claimant come of age); children are records only until they are grown, and only a grown claimant is ever a figure.

## The order of building

Each step is to be playable by itself.

1. A soldier costs a person. No ageing up inside a campaign battle.
2. Towns keep their people and stores between seasons; notable people are kept by name.
3. A season passes in every town: births, deaths, harvest, creed and acts of God, run coarsely.
4. Threats on the map that choose a town and strike it, settled by the town defending itself, with
   a report of what happened to whom.
5. Drift under pressure and the leader's decrees.
